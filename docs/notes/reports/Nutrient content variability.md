# Same food, different numbers: what drives it

> **Superseded (2026-10-22)** by [Nutrient content variability in depth](Nutrient%20content%20variability%20in%20depth.md), whose claims were fact-checked against primary sources. Known error here: the "96% of US snack labels within 20%" attribution to Jumpertz 2013 is not in that paper (it found energy +4.3% above label, median, n = 24).

The same food really does differ in composition from one sample to the next, and the size of that difference depends mostly on which nutrient you look at. **Energy, carbohydrate and protein in plant foods are fairly stable**, usually within about ±10–20% between samples. Retail bananas are a good example: their total sugar ranges from about 13 to 20 g/100 g. **Fat varies far more.** It differs by breed in milk, by cut in meat, and by season in fish, where it can change several-fold. **Soil- or exposure-driven micronutrients vary the most**, and selenium and vitamin D can span 30- to 800-fold within a single food. On top of that natural spread comes a second layer of error from labels and databases. Measured energy has averaged 8–18% above what labels state. US and EU law caps that error asymmetrically, which pushes vitamins above label and lets energy run over. The Atwater energy factors themselves can overstate what the body absorbs from some foods by about a third. A useful order of risk for a nutrition tracker is therefore: micronutrients first, then fat and therefore energy in animal foods, then carbohydrate and protein. One caveat applies throughout: this was a cost-limited run. Every number below comes from search summaries, and I did not check any of them against the primary papers. The core per-nutrient coefficients of variation that USDA publishes were not retrieved (see the last section).

## A banana's carbohydrate barely changes as it ripens; only its form does

The best sample-level data found is a study of retail Cavendish bananas at three ripeness stages, with 5–7 samples per stage. **Starch fell from 4.50 to 2.52 to 0.48 g/100 g** as the fruit ripened. **Total sugars stayed at 15.0, 17.1 and 16.7 g/100 g** ([PMC8266066](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8266066/)). Adding starch and sugars gives about 19.5, 19.6 and 17.2 g/100 g of available carbohydrate (my arithmetic). Ripening mostly converts starch into sugar, so a bunch on the counter does not change much in carbohydrate or calories. The spread within a single stage is larger than you might expect. Among "ripe" fruit, **sucrose ranged from 1.15 to 9.40 g/100 g and starch from 1.55 to 5.40**. Total sugars across all retail samples ran from **12.9 to 19.6 g/100 g**, roughly ±20% around the middle. **Moisture ran from 72 to 81 g/100 g**, and since water dilutes everything else, it is a hidden driver of how much carbohydrate a gram of banana holds. Fibre moved more than either. It fell from about 5 to 2 g/100 g in retail fruit and from about 18 g/100 g in unripe fruit in a separate ripening series ([PMC8266066](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8266066/)). That matters for energy because fibre and resistant starch yield fewer calories than sugar does.

## Fat is the macronutrient that varies, and energy follows it

In animal foods, fat drives almost all of the variability in energy. **Jersey milk averaged 5.2% fat and 3.8% protein, against 3.9% and 3.1% for Holstein** ([UVM](https://scholarworks.uvm.edu/items/867970df-8fed-467e-ba2f-39f1980b9461)). Among 60 individual Irish cows, fat was **4.72 ± 1.30%**, which is a coefficient of variation (CV, the standard deviation as a percentage of the mean) of about 28%. Protein was 3.85 ± 0.61%, a CV of about 16% ([Teagasc](https://t-stor.teagasc.ie/entities/publication/57fa2a2f-8cb4-44a1-9df5-2728f39d8442)). Bulk retail milk averages many cows together, so its spread is much smaller than these per-cow figures. The seasonal effect on milk was small and inconsistent from study to study ([SLU](https://stud.epsilon.slu.se/15823/), [Agrojournal](https://www.agrojournal.org/23/06-24.html)). Farmed **Atlantic salmon fillets went from 3–4% to 17–19% fat** over one year, most of it gained between July and November ([Nofima](https://nofima.com/publication/747198/)). For beef, USDA's own sampling treats fat as something that depends on the cut. Rib roast and strip loin carry more lipid than tenderloin and sirloin ([PMC11397233](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11397233/)). Ground beef is modelled as a regression across declared fat levels from 3% to 30% ([USDA ARS](https://ars.usda.gov/northeast-area/beltsville-md-bhnrc/beltsville-human-nutrition-research-center/methods-and-application-of-food-composition-laboratory/mafcl-site-pages/beef-study-usda-select-and-usda-choice-cuts)). The only general CVs found are old assumed values: **energy 10–30%, protein 10–20%, and other nutrients 10–45%**. The same source notes that USDA publishes no CV for energy because it computes energy from the macronutrients instead of measuring it ([NAP](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/618/chapter/17)).

## Micronutrients swing by orders of magnitude because soil and light set them

Vitamins and minerals vary for reasons outside the plant or animal's own biology: soil, light, feed and storage. **Median selenium in Brazil nuts ranges from 2.07 mg/kg in Mato Grosso to 68.15 mg/kg in Amazonas**, a 33-fold difference driven by selenium in the soil and by soil acidity ([Chemosphere 2017](https://repositorio.unesp.br/handle/11449/163410?show=full)). Individual nuts range from below 0.55 to 362 mg/kg ([abstract](https://scisoc.confex.com/crops/2016am/webprogram/Paper100561.html)). As a result, **USDA and the UK tables disagree about 7-fold** on selenium in Brazil nuts (1.92 vs 0.254 mg/100 g) ([Hodmedods](https://hodmedods.co.uk/blogs/news/selenium-in-brazil-nuts-good-but-highly-variable)). Mushrooms show the same pattern with light. **Vitamin D2 in button mushrooms runs from 0.2 to 164 µg/100 g depending on UV-B dose**, while untreated mushrooms have 0.2–1.5 µg/100 g ([Food & Nutrition Research](https://foodandnutritionresearch.net/index.php/fnr/article/view/498)). Milk iodine is more moderate but still noisy. In Swiss retail milk it was **71 ± 25 µg/L for organic and 111 ± 26 µg/L for conventional**, CVs of about 25–35%. Monthly means ran from 42 to 145 µg/L and were lowest from late summer to autumn. Mineral supplements and iodine teat disinfectants also raise it ([Agroscope](https://ira.agroscope.ch/de-CH/publication/37387), [ETH Zurich](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/247182)). The organic-versus-conventional gap did not replicate in US Northeast milk, where season was the significant factor ([SARE](https://projects.sare.org/media/pdf/T/a/b/Tables-11-13.pdf)).

Vitamin C in produce depends on genotype, light (more light means more vitamin C), nitrogen fertilisation (more nitrogen means less), maturity at harvest, and storage time and temperature ([Lee & Kader 2000](https://ucanr.edu/datastoreFiles/608-371.pdf)). Davis and colleagues compared USDA tables from 1950 and 1999 for 43 crops. They found **median declines of 6–38%** in protein, calcium, phosphorus, iron, riboflavin and vitamin C, and attributed them to breeding for yield, the "dilution effect" ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/soil-depletion-and-nutrition-loss/)). That comparison confounds changes in analytical methods and in the cultivars sampled, so it is suggestive but not conclusive. No source found compared micronutrient CVs directly with macronutrient CVs. The claim that micronutrients are "more variable" therefore rests on these extreme ranges, not on a head-to-head study.

## Labels and databases add their own skewed error

Different providers disagree for two reasons. Their products differ, and the rules give their error a direction. Urban et al. measured **29 restaurant items at about 18% above their stated calories**, with single items reaching 200% of the stated value. **Ten frozen supermarket meals came in about 8% above label** ([Tufts](https://now.tufts.edu/2010/01/06/study-examines-calorie-information-restaurants-packaged-foods), [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/01/100105100021.htm)). Because the spread was so wide, neither gap was statistically significant. A review citing Jumpertz 2013 reports that **96% of US snack labels were within 20% of the measured energy** ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/44E6DBCA1388414E5201EBF0C67C23FD/core-reader)). The bias has a regulatory cause. Under US rule 21 CFR 101.9(g), **calories, sugars, fat and sodium may run up to 120% of the label**. Naturally occurring nutrients must reach at least 80% of the label, and added nutrients at least 100% ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/guidance-industry-guide-developing-and-using-data-bases-nutrition-labeling)). The EU's 2012 guidance allows **vitamins +50%/−35%** ([FSAI](https://www.FSAI.ie/getmedia/71c87819-77a7-4bf2-b741-2f11cf019c2d/EU-guidance-tolerances.pdf)). Manufacturers therefore add extra vitamins so the product stays above the floor through its shelf life, and a modest calorie overrun is legal.

Different databases agree reasonably well on macronutrients and poorly on micronutrients. A EuroFIR comparison of Greek, Belgian and US tables found protein, carbohydrate and fat close, but **vitamins A and C, calcium and iron far apart**. It attributed the gaps to data sources, analytical methods, food origin and coding conventions ([EuroFIR](https://www.eurofir.org/risks-of-using-the-wrong-food-composition-databases-in-nutrition-research/)). Intake estimates computed from British and from American tables differed by **5.3–8.9%** for macronutrients ([Public Health Nutrition](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/5D246886C6B5CC6A44ACF3A6C6F3A2EE)).

Finally, the energy figure itself is a model and not a measurement. When digestion was measured, almonds yielded **4.6 ± 0.8 kcal/g against the 6.0–6.1 kcal/g predicted by Atwater factors**. The Atwater factors are the standard 4/4/9 kcal per gram of carbohydrate, protein and fat, and here they overstated the energy by 32% ([Novotny 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3396444)). Grinding almonds into butter released more of their energy ([Food & Function 2016](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2016/fo/c6fo01076h)). The cause is intact cell walls that hold back fat from digestion. Whole nuts, whole grains and legumes may therefore deliver less than their label says, partly offsetting the upward bias in measured-versus-label energy.

## What this run could not establish

The researchers did not retrieve USDA SR Legacy or FoodData Central sample statistics. Those tables publish n, standard error, minimum and maximum per nutrient. The CV can be computed from them as SD = SE·√n and CV = SD/mean, which would give real per-food spreads for potato, rice, apple, egg, pork and others. The run also found no direct comparison of macronutrient against micronutrient variability, and no Greenfield & Southgate or FAO/INFOODS rule of thumb for how uncertain a table value is. It found no numbers on brand-to-brand variability within one food or on Open Food Facts' crowdsourced data. Figures for vitamin C loss in storage, carotenoids by cultivar, folate in greens, wild-versus-farmed vitamin D in salmon, and the EU energy and macronutrient tolerances are also missing. The Jumpertz 2013 primary paper could not be retrieved, and the Davis 2004 data was seen only through secondary coverage.

## Conclusion

Variability in food composition is not a single error bar. For carbohydrate and protein in plant foods, a ±10–20% band is defensible. For fat and therefore energy in meat, fish and dairy, the uncertainty is roughly a factor of two unless the cut, breed or declared fat level is known. For soil- or light-driven micronutrients such as selenium, iodine and vitamin D, a single table value is close to meaningless for any one item. The biases are not symmetric either. Labels tend to understate energy and overstate the vitamin floor, and Atwater factors overstate energy for intact high-fibre foods. A tracker that gives every nutrient the same symmetric percentage error would understate the uncertainty of micronutrients and fat, and would ignore known directional biases. The cheapest next step is to compute CVs directly from the SR Legacy n/SE fields, which would replace most of the assumed numbers here with measured ones.

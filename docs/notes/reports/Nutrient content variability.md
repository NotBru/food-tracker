# Fat, soil and labels drive nutrient uncertainty

The same food does vary in composition per gram, but how much depends far more on *which nutrient* you look at than on which food. Across up to about 2,700 foods per nutrient (about 3,300 foods in all) in USDA's SR28 database, the typical sample-to-sample coefficient of variation (CV, the standard deviation as a share of the mean) is **about 3% for water, 7% for protein and starch, 12% for sugars, 16% for fibre and 20% for total fat**. Most minerals run **10–20%** (selenium, at 32%, is the exception), and most vitamins run **16–34%**, with selenium, carotenoids and vitamin C at the top and long right tails. On the banana question, yes, it changes, but mostly in form rather than amount. A banana's water content is tight (71–78 g/100 g). Its sugar and starch each swing two- to threefold with ripeness (USDA: sugars 7.5–16.2 g, starch 3.0–9.4 g per 100 g), but they trade off against each other, so available carbohydrate moves much less. Plantains, by contrast, carry roughly 1.4 times as much carbohydrate as dessert bananas (USDA: 31.9 vs 22.8 g/100 g). Between "providers", the biggest jumps come from definitions rather than from fruit. Carbohydrate "by difference" (USDA's 22.8 g) includes about 2.6 g of fibre, while "available carbohydrate" tables exclude it, a gap of roughly 11%. Layered on top of natural variation are three further sources of error. Labels are allowed one-sided error and on average understate energy by a few percent. Brands within one generic food differ more than any label errs. And the energy figure is itself a model that overstates what people absorb from whole nuts by 5–32%. A nutrition tracker should therefore give each nutrient its own CV, make that CV depend on the food group and on how the food was identified, apply known biases as shifts rather than as symmetric noise, and widen the band to an order of magnitude for selenium in plant foods, iodine and vitamin D.

## Sample-to-sample CVs climb from 3% for water to 34% for vitamin C

The most systematic evidence comes from computing CVs directly from USDA's SR28 nutrient file, which publishes, for each food and nutrient, the number of analyses (n), the standard error of the mean (SE), and for newer data the minimum and maximum. The research team derived CV = SE·√n / mean for every food–nutrient pair with n ≥ 5 ([USDA SR28 ASCII release](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400525/Data/SR/SR28/dnload/sr28asc.zip); computations in `data/sr28_cv_by_nutrient.csv`). The ranking is stable (rank correlation ≥ 0.95, though sugars rise from 0.09 to 0.20 as the threshold tightens) whether the threshold is n ≥ 3 or n ≥ 10, and whether all records or only single-study analytical records are used.

| Median CV across foods (n ≥ 5) | Nutrients | Share of foods with CV > 0.5 |
|---|---|---|
| **0.03** | water | 0.8% |
| **0.07** | starch, protein | 1.6% (protein) |
| **0.10** | magnesium, phosphorus | — |
| **0.12** | total sugars, potassium | 14% (sugars) |
| **0.15–0.17** | zinc, sodium, fibre, vitamin B6, niacin, manganese | 7–17% |
| **0.18–0.21** | riboflavin, iron, calcium, **total fat**, vitamin E, copper, B12, thiamin | 9–17% |
| **0.23–0.27** | folate, β-carotene | 22–32% |
| **0.31–0.34** | vitamin K1, selenium, vitamin A (RAE), vitamin C | 26–31% |

The headline pattern is that **micronutrients are roughly twice as variable as macronutrients, but much of that gap is an artefact of water**. Within the same food, the median micronutrient CV exceeded the median macronutrient CV in 83% of 1,932 foods (ratio 1.9×). Excluding water, whose CV is structurally tiny, the ratio falls to 1.2×. It is about 1.0× in fruit, vegetables, meat and fish, and stays at 1.6–1.9× in nuts, grains and dairy (about 1.3× in legumes) ([SR28](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400525/Data/SR/SR28/dnload/sr28asc.zip)). The reason the macro and micro distributions overlap in produce and meat is fat. Fat is present only in traces in fruit and vegetables, where its median CV is 0.41, and it tracks trim, marbling and fattening in meat and fish, where the median is 0.25. When the macronutrient that actually carries a food's energy is examined, it is tight. Starch in grains has a CV of about 0.02–0.06 (median 0.04), fat in almonds, Brazil nuts, cheddar and egg 0.03–0.05 (group medians about 0.08), protein in meat and dairy 0.03–0.09, and apple sugars 0.05.

Individual foods illustrate the spread. Brazil nuts have protein and fat CVs of 0.03, yet selenium ranges from **136 to 2,740 µg/100 g** across 15 analyses, a 20-fold spread. Carrot β-carotene spans 1,990–21,000 µg (n = 197). Almond fat has a CV of 0.05 while its riboflavin and folate CVs are about 0.95. Egg and cheddar macronutrients are all at 0.06 or below ([SR28](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400525/Data/SR/SR28/dnload/sr28asc.zip)).

Three caveats limit how literally these numbers can be used. First, **SR28 publishes almost no variability for energy or carbohydrate**. Both are calculated (n = 0) in 95% of foods and carry an SE in only about ten, vitamin A is calculated in most foods, and 68% of all rows in the file are calculated or imputed rather than analysed ([SR28 documentation](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400525/Data/SR/SR28/sr28_doc.pdf)). Energy uncertainty must therefore be propagated from its components. Second, the "n" counts analyses, which are often **composites of several units**. USDA's national sampling plan typically pools 12 retail locations into four composites ([Trainer et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079229)). Pooling three units shrinks the apparent SD by about √3 ≈ 1.7×, so for composite-based foods SE·√n understates the spread between single items. Third, **9.3% of checkable records report an SE that implies an SD larger than the published min–max range allows**. That rate rises to 25% for multi-study records, where the SE reflects between-study scatter under USDA's "generic mean" procedure. Restricting to range-consistent records lowers the medians by about a third (fat 0.12, iron 0.135, protein 0.057) but leaves the ranking unchanged. A range-based measure, (max − min)/mean, gives the same order: water 0.08, protein 0.18, fat 0.41, iron 0.46, selenium 0.89, vitamin C 1.00 ([SR28](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400525/Data/SR/SR28/dnload/sr28asc.zip)). Any single food's CV is therefore good to roughly a factor of two, while the group-level medians are robust.

Analytical error is not what drives these numbers. In homogeneity checks of NFNAP control composites, the CV across aliquots was "usually less than 1%" (up to about 6% for a composite run at a contract laboratory) ([Phillips et al. 2006](https://www.ars.usda.gov/SP2UserFiles/Place/12354500/Articles/AnalBioaanlChem_QCPaper.pdf)). The Horwitz relation predicts between-laboratory error of about 3% at 10 g/100 g, rising to about 8% at 10 mg/100 g and 16% at 0.1 mg/100 g ([Horwitz & Albert 2006](https://doi.org/10.1093/jaoac/89.4.1095)). So for macronutrients, natural and sampling variation dominates, while for trace nutrients analytical error contributes a real share. The exceptions are fat and fibre. When 19 leading laboratories analysed identical homogenised foods, their between-laboratory CVs were **2.8–6.4% for protein but 5.4–54% for fat, 9–27% for available carbohydrate and 23–84% for dietary fibre** ([Hollman & Katan 1988](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0002-8223(21)02007-1)). That is a reminder that fibre values differ partly because of method, not food.

## A banana's carbohydrate holds steady while its sugar–starch split moves

USDA's "Bananas, raw" entry shows the pattern clearly. **Water is 74.9 g/100 g with a range of 71.3–78.2 g (n = 20, CV 0.017)**. **Total sugars are 12.2 g (range 7.51–16.21, CV 0.24) and starch 5.4 g (range 3.00–9.39, CV 0.30)**, but both rest on only 8 analyses from 2 studies and were last updated in 2002. Within the sugars, sucrose alone has a CV of 0.65. Fibre is 2.6 g (1.7–3.7, CV 0.18). Carbohydrate by difference (22.84 g) and energy (89 kcal) are calculated values with no SE, minimum or maximum ([SR28](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400525/Data/SR/SR28/dnload/sr28asc.zip); `data/sr28_banana_detail.csv`). The wide sugar and starch ranges are most plausibly ripeness, which SR28 does not record. A separate retail study of Cavendish bananas supports that reading. As fruit went from slightly ripe to overripe, **starch fell from 4.50 to 0.48 g/100 g while total sugars went 15.0 → 17.1 → 16.7 g**. Starch plus sugars therefore stayed at about 19.5, 19.6 and 17.2 g (my arithmetic), while moisture rose to 78.6 g in overripe fruit and fibre measured by the newer AOAC 2011.25 method, which captures resistant starch, fell from about 5.0 to 1.9 g, while the older AOAC 991.43 method gave a steady 1.6–1.8 g ([Phillips et al. 2021, PMC8266066](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8266066/)). The practical consequence is that **total available carbohydrate in a dessert banana varies perhaps ±10%, while sugar and starch individually vary ±25–30%**. Because sugar and starch move in opposite directions, adding their separate uncertainties in quadrature would overstate the uncertainty of the total.

Moving from one cultivar group to another is a far bigger step than ripening. In a Colombian ripening trial, pulp dry matter and starch ranked plantain (Dominico Harton) above cooking banana (Guineo) above dessert banana (Gros Michel). Plantains also hydrolysed starch more slowly and retained a little at full ripeness (about 3.4% vs 0.02% for Gros Michel) ([Moreno et al. 2021, PMC7984252](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7984252)). That table is internally inconsistent, since it reports starch above dry matter on the same wet basis, so only the ranking is usable. Reviews put plantain starch at up to about 35% of pulp ([Cordenunsi-Lysenko et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2019.00391/full)), and USDA gives carbohydrate by difference of 31.9 g/100 g for plantain against 22.8 g for banana, so plantain pulp carries roughly **1.4× the carbohydrate per gram of a dessert banana** ([SR28](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400525/Data/SR/SR28/dnload/sr28asc.zip)). We found no usable data comparing Cavendish composition by country of origin or by length of cold-chain storage. "Provider" effects for fresh bananas are therefore unquantified beyond cultivar and ripeness.

Water is the hidden lever behind per-gram variation in all fresh produce. Any change in dry matter scales every solid component, energy included, by the same factor. Potato dry matter differed between cultivars from about 20.8% (Almera) to 25.5% (Agria) over three seasons in trials in Montenegro, 2015–2017 ([Plantarum repository](https://plantarum.izbis.bg.ac.rs/handle/123456789/675); unverified, figures from a search snippet because the full text could not be opened). That implies about 23% more starch, protein and energy per 100 g fresh weight even with identical dry-matter composition. In Dutch trials, cultivar explained only 10–30% of variance in tuber quality traits, location and year most of the rest, and tubers within one lot varied more than lots did ([AGRIS record of Veerman 2001](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/6511ad910777009829fa331d); unverified, from a search summary of the abstract because the record could not be opened). SR28 reflects this, giving potato starch a CV of 0.33. For a vegetable with 5–10% dry matter, a 1-point shift in dry matter is a 10–20% relative change in everything else.

Protein in grains and legumes responds more to management than to genetics. In Korean brown rice, nitrogen rate moved protein from **5.7% to 6.8%**, and late transplanting raised it from 5.7% to 6.6%. Nitrogen explained 38.8% of the variance and variety only 8.2% ([Lee et al. 2022](https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/65bbb72540e64bb074ab0ce1)). In 40 European wheat genotypes, genotype explained about half of nitrogen-corrected grain-N variance and genotype × site-year about a sixth ([Mosleth et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7397848)). The FAO/INFOODS handbook notes that cereal protein "can vary by a factor of two, depending on variety and fertilizer usage" ([Greenfield & Southgate 2003, ch. 11](https://www.fao.org/4/y4705e/y4705e16.htm)). Among released faba bean cultivars, protein ranged 23.9–31.6% of dry matter with a CV of about 6%, and genotype dominated ([Greveniotis et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10648669/)). Storage changes the form of carbohydrate more than its amount. Potatoes held at 4 °C accumulate glucose and fructose (cold-induced sweetening, driven mainly by invertase breakdown of sucrose; starch content barely changed), and how much depends strongly on cultivar ([Galani Yamdeu et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/161257/)). That shift matters for glycaemic response, not for grams of carbohydrate.

## In animal foods, fat swings energy by a third or more

Protein in lean animal tissue sits in a narrow band, and fat and water trade places, so a fat estimate predicts most of the energy variation. In 180 retail USDA Prime beef products, raw separable lean held **21.7–23.2% protein across cuts, while lipid ran from 6.5% (top sirloin) to 12.7% (rib roast)**. Calculated energy therefore ranged from 151 to 202 kcal/100 g, **a 33% spread within one grade** (measured from the leanest cut) ([Mortensen et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11397233/)). Trimming dwarfs that. External and seam fat made up **about 4% of a tenderloin but 28% of a rib roast** by weight, and whether a user eats it decides the fat they actually consume ([Mortensen et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11397233/)). SR28 gives lean beef and pork cuts fat CVs of 0.21–0.31, against protein CVs of 0.03–0.09. Many retail beef entries carry analysed proximates but imputed micronutrients (n = 0), so micronutrient uncertainty for those cuts is unknown rather than small ([SR28](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400525/Data/SR/SR28/dnload/sr28asc.zip)).

Fish fat cycles with season, spawning and farming. In Norwegian samples, **farmed Atlantic salmon muscle held 8.97% lipid against 2.14% for wild fish (n = 3 fish each), yet EPA + DHA was about equal at roughly 520 mg/100 g**, because the extra farmed fat comes from vegetable-oil-based feed (mostly oleic, linoleic and α-linolenic acids), which dilutes EPA + DHA to about a third of the share it holds in wild fish ([Molversmyr et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9469852)). Farmed salmon fillets went from 3–4% to 17–19% fat over a single year of growth ([Mørkøre & Rørvik 2001](https://nofima.com/publication/747198/)). SR28 gives wild Atlantic salmon a fat CV of 0.74 (n = 7) against 0.19 for farmed (n = 4). Norwegian spring-spawning herring arrives at its wintering grounds at about 21–22% fat and loses about 10 percentage points through migration and spawning ([Slotte 1996, ICES CM 1996/H:11](https://hdl.handle.net/11250/105591)). For herring, that implies roughly 160 to 340 kcal/100 g depending on catch date (my estimate: about 18 g protein × 4 kcal/g plus 10–30% fat × 9 kcal/g). Widely repeated claims of mackerel ranging from under 5% to 28% fat trace to a promotional page and are left aside here.

Dairy shows how pooling tames variation. In individual-cow milk, fat is the most variable component. Across 1,035 morning and evening samples from 60 Irish Jersey and Holstein–Friesian cows over nine months, fat was 4.72 ± 1.30% (CV about 28%) and protein 3.85 ± 0.61% (CV about 16%) ([Parmar et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods9081004)). Breed shifts the mean: Jersey milk averaged 5.2% fat against 3.9% for Holstein in one herd ([O'Day 2014, UVM undergraduate thesis](https://scholarworks.uvm.edu/items/867970df-8fed-467e-ba2f-39f1980b9461)). Retail milk blends many cows and is standardised to a declared fat level, and SR28 accordingly gives whole milk fat and protein CVs of only 0.04–0.05, and cheddar 0.03–0.04. Eggs are similarly tight in SR28 (protein, fat and water ≤ 0.05), though within each egg size class hen age raises yolk share ([Akbar et al. 1983](https://doi.org/10.3382/ps.0620925)), and with it, presumably, fat per gram; we found no study quantifying that per 100 g.

## Storage, soil and sunlight push micronutrients two- to seventyfold

Micronutrients fall into three behavioural classes, and the right uncertainty model differs for each. **Labile vitamins (C, folate, carotenoids in leaves) decline directionally with time and temperature after harvest.** Lee and Kader call postharvest temperature "the most important factor" for vitamin C. Kale lost **2–5% of its vitamin C in two days at 0 °C but 61–89% at 20 °C**, with wilting accelerating the loss. Vegetables also differ in how well they hold vitamin C. Broccoli and Brussels sprouts retain over 95%, peas, spinach and turnip 65–70%, and asparagus and green beans only 5–30% ([Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)). Packaged spinach kept only **53% of its folate and 44–61% of its carotenoids** by the end of its visual shelf life, at 4, 10 or 20 °C ([Pandrangi & LaBorde 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2621.2004.tb09919.x)). Blanching broccoli for freezing cost 41–42% of vitamin C, and a year in the freezer another 15–18% ([Lisiewska & Kmiecik 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/0308-8146(95)00218-9)). Apples stored at 0 °C for six months fell from 10.2 to 4.2 mg/100 g (Delicious) ([Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)). Because these losses only go one way, a table value measured near harvest is a biased estimate for produce eaten after a week in the fridge.

**Genotype-driven micronutrients vary two- to fourfold across cultivars at harvest.** Strawberry vitamin C ranged 19.3–71.5 mg/100 g across six cultivars and four locations, and pepper 64–168 mg/100 g ([Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)). Orange carrots spanned **4.6–10.3 mg β-carotene/100 g across 19 cultivars** ([Heinonen 1990](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf00093a005)), and sweet potato carotenoids run from trace to over 9 mg/100 g depending on flesh colour ([Hagenimana et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1080/03670244.1998.9991560)). Spinach folate ranged 54–173 µg/100 g across 67 accessions grown under identical conditions ([Shohag et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf203442h)). Origin matters too: California oranges averaged 83.2 mg vitamin C/100 g against 63.0 for Florida ([Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)). Tomato lycopene is the counterexample, with a broad-sense heritability of only 5.8%, so growing environment (and, presumably, ripeness) dominates ([Panthee et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10681-013-0895-1)). Light pushes leafy-green nutrients in opposite directions. Shade nets cut ascorbic acid in April-sown baby spinach by 12–33% (the August sowing responded inconsistently), and often raised carotenoids ([Bergquist et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf070396z)).

**Exposure-driven micronutrients (selenium, iodine, vitamin D, B12) vary by an order of magnitude or more, because the soil, feed or light sets them.** Median selenium in Brazil nuts ranged from **2.07 mg/kg in Mato Grosso to 68.15 mg/kg in Amazonas**, so one nut supplies anywhere from 11% to 288% of a day's requirement ([Silva Junior et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemosphere.2017.08.158)). Finland's addition of selenium to fertiliser from 1985 raised spring-cereal selenium about 15-fold, beef 6-fold and milk 3-fold. Organic plant foods remained broadly at pre-1985 levels ([Alfthan et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtemb.2014.04.009)). Wheat grain selenium responds to soil rather than to genotype ([Zhao et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcs.2008.11.007)). UK retail organic milk carried **32–44% less iodine than conventional**, with smaller gaps in summer ([Payling et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2015.01.091); [Stevenson et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2017.06.135)). In Norway, iodine in wild fish ran from 18 µg/100 g (halibut) to 1,210 µg/100 g (pollack) ([Nerhus et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.29219/fnr.v62.1291)). Farmed salmon had about a quarter of wild salmon's vitamin D, and within-species spread was enormous: wild salmon averaged 988 ± 524 IU (mean ± SEM) per 3.5 oz (≈100 g) across nine fish, which implies an SD of roughly 1,500 IU ([Lu et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2698592/)). Eggs from hens kept outdoors had **3–4 times the yolk vitamin D3** of eggs from hens kept indoors, in a 4-week trial, though supermarket free-range eggs were low ([Kühn et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nut.2013.10.002)). Button mushrooms reached 6.6–23 µg vitamin D2 per gram of solids after minutes of UV-C ([Koyyalamudi et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf803908q)). Milk B12 ranged **0.7–9.0 ng/mL across 3,533 Holstein cows**, driven by diet fibre and genetics (heritability 0.37) ([Duplessis et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3168/jdsc.2023-0535)).

The "nutrient decline" literature belongs here as context, not as a correction factor. Comparisons of USDA tables from 1950 and 1999 found median declines from 6% (protein) to 38% (riboflavin) across 43 crops, but the authors themselves note that most individual-food ratios "are usually not distinguishable from 1" ([Davis et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1080/07315724.2004.10719409); abstract only). UK tables show iron, copper and sodium falling about 50% in fruit and vegetables from 1940 to 2019 on a dry-weight basis ([Mayer et al. 2021](https://pure.coventry.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/45908836/Mayer_et_al_Historical_changes_mineral_content.pdf)). Marles calls such table comparisons "not reliable" because methods, varieties and sampling all changed. He notes that archived soils show no mineral depletion, and that the solid evidence is a yield-driven "dilution effect" seen when old and new cultivars are grown side by side ([Marles 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfca.2016.11.012)). Archived Broadbalk wheat confirms that dilution: grain zinc, iron, copper and magnesium fell after semi-dwarf cultivars arrived, even though soil levels held steady ([Fan et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtemb.2008.07.002)). For a tracker, the lesson is that **old table values for minerals in produce tend to sit high**, but by an amount too uncertain to correct.

## Labels, brands and databases add a skewed second layer

The label on a packaged food is accurate on average for energy but leans in a predictable direction. In 24 US snack foods, measured metabolisable energy ran a **median 4.3% above the label (IQR 0.2–13.7%)**, with 55% of the excess from heavier-than-stated servings and 40% from extra carbohydrate. Individual macronutrient errors were large: carbohydrate deviations ran from −1.6% to +157%, and fat from −59% to +87%, in a 10-item subsample ([Jumpertz et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3605747/)). Ten reduced-energy US frozen meals averaged +8% (SD 14%; not significant) ([Urban et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2838242/)). Across 269 restaurant items, the mean error was only +10 kcal per portion, yet **19% of items were at least 100 kcal above their stated value**, and low-calorie items were systematically understated ([Urban et al. 2011](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21771989)). Canadian inspection found 16.7% of over 1,000 products outside ±20% of label, and **every one of those failures contained more of the nutrient than declared**. Sodium failed most often (18.4%), then saturated fat (15.8%), calories (14.2%) and sugar (12.9%) ([Fitzpatrick et al. 2014](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25153971)). Spanish processed foods met EU sugar tolerances 98.4% of the time ([Yusta-Boyo et al. 2020](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32295050)). Fortified foods are less predictable. Only 29% of US milk samples held 80–120% of their labelled vitamin D, while infant formulas reached up to 419% ([Holick et al. 1992](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/1313548)), and USDA's Dietary Supplement Ingredient Database predicts overages above 20% of label in 50–99% of multivitamin-minerals for iodine and selenium (adult products) and for iodine and vitamin D (children's products) ([Andrews et al. 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31505677)).

The regulations explain the skew. Under US law, calories, sugars, fat and sodium are misbranded only above 120% of the label, and running under is a "reasonable deficiency". Naturally occurring protein, carbohydrate, fibre, vitamins and minerals must reach 80% of the label, and added nutrients 100% ([21 CFR 101.9(g)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.9)). EU guidance is symmetric for macronutrients: ±20% in the middle range, but fixed grams at low and high levels (±2 g of carbohydrate, sugar, protein or fibre below 10 g/100 g, which is ±67% for a food with 3 g of sugar). It allows **+50%/−35% for vitamins and +45%/−35% for minerals** in foods other than supplements. It sets no tolerance for energy, and it requires compliance "during the entire shelf life", which in practice encourages manufacturers to dose vitamins high at production ([EC tolerance guidance 2012](https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2016-10/labelling_nutrition-vitamins_minerals-guidance_tolerances_1212_en.pdf)). EU rules let label values come from analysis, recipe calculation or generally accepted data. US rules do not require labels to be derived from analysis, since compliance is judged by FDA testing, and they explicitly allow FDA-approved databases ([21 CFR 101.9(g)(8)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.9)). The US also allows five calorie calculation methods plus fixed factors for sugar alcohols ([21 CFR 101.9(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.9)).

Choosing the wrong brand costs more than trusting the right brand's label. UK sliced bread averaged 0.98 ± 0.13 g salt/100 g across 203 products, a CV of about 13% ([Brinsden et al. 2013](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23794567)). UK fruit yoghurts ranged from **4.6 to 21.3 g sugar/100 g** ([Moore et al. 2018](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30228100)), and 40% of yoghurt products surveyed in 2019 could not be matched by brand and name to any product from 2016 ([Moore et al. 2020](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31936185)). The same Chicken McNuggets held 12 g fat/100 g in Germany and 21.1 g in New Zealand ([Ziauddeen et al. 2015](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25702788)), and 0.6 g salt/100 g in the UK against 1.6 g in the US ([Dunford et al. 2012](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22508978)).

Reference databases agree closely on energy for whole diets but diverge on definitions. EPIC found nine European tables "not comparable" for folate and fibre (different methods) and for energy, protein, carbohydrate, carotenes and vitamins A and E (different modes of expression) ([Deharveng et al. 1999](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/10048800)). Counting fibre energy changed the energy of **321 of 1,753 Brazilian foods by 10% or more**, mostly vegetables, legumes and fruit, but changed whole daily menus by under 3% ([Menezes et al. 2016](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26433298)). Korean table estimates matched chemically analysed duplicate diets to within 2% for energy, but were 8% low for carbohydrate and 24% high for fat ([Kim et al. 2003](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12862306)). Borrowing another country's table is risky: UK and US tables overstated fat from meat in the Australian food supply by about 60% (and total fat by 15–22%) ([Greenfield & Southgate 2003, ch. 11](https://www.fao.org/4/y4705e/y4705e16.htm)). Crowdsourced entries are the weakest link. Cleaned MyFitnessPal diaries correlated with a Belgian reference at r = 0.96 for energy but only about 0.5 for sodium and cholesterol, and 2.8% of entries had to be discarded as implausible ([Evenepoel et al. 2020](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/33084583)). Within the fruit group (15 of 50 common foods), MyFitnessPal's agreement with a research database fell to an intraclass correlation (ICC) of **0.33–0.43** for calories, carbohydrate and fibre, whereas CalorieKing agreed at 0.90 or above across all foods ([Lin et al. 2020](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC7258431), conference abstract). We found no peer-reviewed audit of Open Food Facts accuracy, and the "15–27% crowdsourced error" figures circulating online appear to trace to a vendor blog with faulty citations (not re-verified).

## Energy is itself a model with 5–30% built-in error

Every calorie figure in a database or on a label is computed from macronutrients using conversion factors. Those factors assume constant digestibility across foods and people, which the FAO/INFOODS handbook calls "questionable assumptions" ([Greenfield & Southgate 2003, ch. 9](https://www.fao.org/4/y4705e/y4705e14.htm)). The choice of factor system matters only modestly for mixed diets. Specific versus general Atwater factors shift diet energy by about −3% to +2% (up to about 5% in one US comparison), and switching to net metabolisable energy lowers it by 4–6%. For individual high-protein or high-fibre foods, though, the shift reaches 24–27%, and FAO concludes that "in most cases the error incurred will be about 5 percent" ([FAO 2003](https://www.fao.org/4/y5022e/y5022e04.htm)). Carbohydrate by difference, multiplied by 4 kcal/g, counts fibre at 4 kcal/g instead of about 2. For a food with 10 g of fibre per 100 g, that adds about 20 kcal/100 g (my arithmetic).

Controlled human feeding studies show where the model fails. Atwater factors overstated the energy people absorbed from **almonds by 32% (4.6 vs 6.0–6.1 kcal/g), walnuts by 21%, cashews by 16% and pistachios by 5%** ([Novotny et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3396444/); [Baer et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.3945/jn.115.217372); [Baer & Novotny 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6356908/); [Baer et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114511002649)). Food form changed absorption almost as much as composition did. Whole natural almonds yielded 4.42 kcal/g, chopped 5.04, and almond butter 6.53, which matches the Atwater prediction ([Gebauer et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1039/c6fo01076h)). People differ too. Individual almond values ranged from **2.2 to 6.0 kcal/g** (between-person SD about 17% of the mean), while the same person repeated within a 6% CV ([Novotny et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3396444/)). Across whole diets, people absorbed 94–97% of a Western diet's energy but **84–96% of a high-fibre, minimally processed diet's** ([Corbin et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10232526/)). For refined foods, therefore, energy absorption is predictable to within a few percent. For whole nuts, intact grains and legumes, the label figure is biased high and the person-to-person spread is wide.

## A per-nutrient uncertainty budget for a tracker

The evidence supports a layered model rather than a single error bar. The first layer is the **natural item-to-item SD** of a well-identified food, which should come from the full spread, not the SE of the table mean. The second is **identification error**: generic versus branded entries, cultivar, cut, trim and ripeness. The third is **source error**: label tolerance and bias, crowdsourced entries, and definitional mismatches. The fourth applies to energy alone: **absorption error**. The table below turns the evidence into default single-item CVs for a weighed portion matched to a good generic database entry. These are synthesis judgements anchored on the SR28 medians and the studies above, not values any single source publishes.

| Nutrient | Default single-item CV | Raise it when | Directional shifts to model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | 3% | fresh produce of unknown ripeness | — |
| Protein | 7–10% (animal, dairy); 15% (produce) | cereals of unknown origin (N fertiliser) | — |
| Starch / sugars (each) | 7–12%; **25–30% in ripening fruit** | ripeness unknown; potato after cold storage | sugar and starch anti-correlate: total carbohydrate is tighter than the parts |
| Total carbohydrate | ~10% (propagated) | database definition unknown | by-difference vs available differs by the fibre content (banana ≈ 11%) |
| Fibre | 16–25% | always add method uncertainty (between-lab 23–84%) | — |
| Fat | 8% (dairy, egg, nuts); **25% (meat, fish)**; 40% (produce, trace) | cut, trim, wild vs farmed or season unknown → treat as a range, not a CV | — |
| Energy | propagate from macros, then add 5% for the factor system | whole nuts, intact grains, high fibre | **shift down 5–32% for whole nuts**; measured energy averages 4–8% above the label |
| Mg, P, K | 10–12% | — | — |
| Zn, Na, Fe, Ca, Cu | 15–20% | generic processed foods (sodium spread between brands) | label sodium tends to run under the true value |
| B vitamins, vitamin E | 17–25% | — | — |
| Folate, carotenoids | 25–30%; lognormal | cultivar or colour type unknown | stored leafy greens lose ~50% by end of shelf life |
| Vitamin C | 35%; lognormal | stored produce | **shift down with storage time and temperature** |
| Selenium (plant foods), iodine, vitamin D | **50–150%; treat as an order-of-magnitude range** | origin, farm system, UV or sun exposure unknown | supplements run above label; fortified foods can miss in either direction (milk vitamin D mostly below label, infant formula above) |
| B12, selenium (animal foods) | 20–30% | animal's diet unknown → widen | — |

Four implementation rules follow from the evidence. First, use **lognormal or skewed intervals** for micronutrients. Their CVs above 0.3 and long right tails (around 30% of foods have CV > 0.5 for vitamin C, β-carotene and selenium) make symmetric ± bands go negative. Second, aggregate correctly across a day. Item-to-item natural variation is independent between foods, so it shrinks roughly with the square root of the number of items. A day of ten foods each at 20% CV has a total CV of about 6% if the foods contribute evenly (20%/√10), and closer to 10–15% if one or two foods dominate. Biases do not shrink in the same way: the Atwater overstatement for nuts, a database's carbohydrate definition, stale micronutrient values, and a mis-chosen brand recur on every log of that food. They should therefore be added linearly per food and carried through over weeks. Third, scale uncertainty to how the food was identified. A generic "fruit yoghurt" should inherit the between-brand spread (several g of sugar per 100 g), while a scanned barcode gets label tolerance plus the modest positive energy bias. A crowdsourced entry deserves the widest band of all, and a sanity filter like Evenepoel's per-portion caps. Fourth, report the uncertainty of an energy total separately for "content" (what the food contains) and "absorbed" (what the body gets). For refined diets the two are close. For nut- and fibre-heavy diets they diverge by 5–10% at the diet level and far more for single foods.

Several gaps remain open. We found no study that directly measured paired macro and micro CVs on the same individual samples. SR28's SE-based CVs are rough per food, often reflect composites, and can be computed for only about ten foods for energy or total carbohydrate. We found no primary data on Cavendish composition by country of origin or cold-chain duration, on brand-to-brand macronutrient or energy spread for cereals, peanut butter or chocolate, or on quantitative accuracy of Open Food Facts entries. The default CVs above are best treated as priors to be refined. A natural refinement is to recompute them food by food from range-consistent SR28 records and from branded-food databases.

## Conclusion

The most important reframing is that a tracker's error budget is dominated not by the banana in front of the user but by *which* entry represents it and by the systematic layers stacked on that entry. Natural sample-to-sample variation in the energy-carrying macronutrients is small for protein and carbohydrate (a few percent to about 12%) and moderate for fat (about 20%, 25% in meat and fish), and it averages out across a day's foods. Definitional choices, unmodelled ripeness, cut and trim, brand substitution and Atwater overestimation for intact plant foods do not average out, and several of them run in a known direction. An app that applies one symmetric ±10% to everything would be too pessimistic about protein and refined carbohydrate. It would also be blind to the steady, one-way energy overstatement in nut-heavy diets and the near-meaninglessness of a single selenium or vitamin D figure.

The same evidence also suggests where effort pays off. Capturing four facts at logging time would remove more uncertainty than any refinement of the underlying tables: ripeness for fruit, cut and trim for meat, wild versus farmed for fish, and brand versus generic for processed foods. The SR28 statistics are good enough to set per-nutrient priors by food group today. Treating them as lower bounds for composite-sampled foods, and as order-of-magnitude ranges for soil- and light-driven micronutrients, gives users error bars that are honest about what any food composition table can know.

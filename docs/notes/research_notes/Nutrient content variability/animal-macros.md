# Variability of energy and macronutrients per 100 g in animal foods, and its causes

Run note: I stopped at the researcher budget of about 15 tool calls. I fetched two primary papers in full: Mortensen et al. 2024 (USDA Prime beef) and Molversmyr et al. 2022 (wild vs farmed salmon). Two PDFs (USDA egg 2010 report; Polish Holstein milk CV paper) downloaded but could not be read, because no PDF text tool was available. Every other number comes from search-result summaries of the cited page. These are marked "(search summary)" and should be checked before anyone quotes them. Values are per raw edible portion unless stated.

## What SDs/CVs/ranges are reported for fat, protein and energy within the same cut or product (beef, pork, chicken, ground meat)?

### Takeaway
Within a beef grade, cut is the main driver of lipid in separable lean: raw USDA Prime separable lean ranges from 6.5% (top sirloin) to 12.7% (rib roast). Trim choice adds far more fat. External plus seam fat makes up about 4-28% of cut weight. Protein in lean stays close to 22% raw and moves much less. Energy follows fat, at roughly 150-200 kcal/100 g raw lean. I found no per-sample SDs for USDA beef, pork or chicken, and no USDA study of declared vs analysed ground beef.

### Cited Findings
- **USDA Prime beef, Mortensen EG, Fuerniss HF, Legako JF, Thompson LD, Woerner DR (2024), *Nutrients* 16(17):2912, DOI 10.3390/nu16172912.** 180 retail products from 6 US cities over 3 collection periods. The analysis uses n = 3 composites per cut and cook type, with a pooled SEM per table, not per-sample SD. Raw separable lean, g/100 g (moisture / protein / lipid):
  - strip loin 65.9 / 21.7 / 11.6
  - tenderloin 69.2 / 21.8 / 9.2
  - top sirloin 70.1 / 23.2 / 6.5
  - rib roast (composited with ribeye) 64.6 / 21.9 / 12.7
  - Pooled SEM: lipid 0.48, moisture 0.39, protein 0.48.
  - Source: [Mortensen et al. 2024, PMC11397233](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11397233/)
- Same paper, cooked separable lean, g/100 g (moisture / protein / lipid):
  - strip 54.7 / 30.7 / 14.0
  - tenderloin 57.2 / 30.9 / 12.1
  - top sirloin 58.5 / 33.1 / 9.2
  - ribeye 53.2 / 30.2 / 16.6
  - rib roast 53.8 / 29.2 / 17.0
  - Pooled SEM: lipid 0.89, moisture 0.87, protein 0.68. Cooking concentrates protein and fat through water loss (moisture falls about 11-12 points).
  - Source: [Mortensen et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11397233/)
- Same paper, energy (calculated as 4 kcal/g protein + 9 kcal/g lipid), raw lean per 100 g: rib roast 201.9, strip 191.2, tenderloin 170.0, top sirloin 151.3 kcal. That is a 33% spread between cuts of one grade — [Mortensen et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11397233/)
- Same paper, dissection of raw cuts, % of cut weight, mean ± SEM:
  - external fat: rib roast 15.7 ± 1.68, strip 12.2 ± 1.06, ribeye 11.2 ± 1.07, sirloin 3.6 ± 0.81, tenderloin 2.3 ± 0.56
  - seam fat: ribeye 13.1 ± 1.26, rib roast 12.2 ± 1.09, strip 4.0, tenderloin 1.3, sirloin 0.5
  - separable lean: 71.6% (rib roast) to 95.9% (tenderloin)
  - Source: [Mortensen et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11397233/)
- USDA Choice and Select beef nationwide study (98 cuts, raw and cooked, 8-48 analytical samples per item). Means and SDs were computed from individual samples, and grade and yield-grade effects were published in a series of *Meat Science* papers from 2013-2015. I did not retrieve the SD values — [USDA ARS documentation](https://ars.usda.gov/ARSUSERFILES/80400535/DATA/BEEF/USDA%20SELECT%20AND%20CHOICE%20BEEF/USDA%20SELECT%20AND%20CHOICE%20BEEF%20CUTS%20DOCUMENTATION.DOCX) (from the earlier lite notes)
- Grain-finished steer loin and round cuts: 4 Upper Choice, 7 Low Choice and 8 Select cuts qualify for the "lean" claim on cooked separable lean. Leanness depends on grade within the same cut (search summary) — [Colorado State thesis](https://mountainscholar.org/handle/10217/80131)
- USDA variety meats study: reports means and SEM from 3 composites per item, not SD (search summary) — [Iowa State Meat and Muscle Biology](https://iastatedigitalpress.com/mmb/article/id/9027)
- Ground beef, declared vs measured: I found no peer-reviewed or USDA study. A local TV lab test (n = 1 package per store, not peer-reviewed) found a "93% lean" package at about 19-20% fat and an "85% lean" package at 23.4% fat. A follow-up found most samples matched or beat their labels (search summary) — [WWLP 22News](https://digital-release.wwlp.com/?p=280090)

### Inferences
- Pooled SEM of about 0.5 g with n = 3 composites implies a between-composite SD of roughly 0.8 g lipid (SEM × √3) for a given Prime cut. That is a CV of about 7-12% of lipid in separable lean. This is my calculation; composites hide the scatter between individual steaks, so true per-steak variability is larger.
- How much fat is actually eaten depends mainly on whether external and seam fat is trimmed or eaten. Separable fat is 4-28% of cut weight and is mostly lipid, so this choice dwarfs the analytical scatter within the lean.

### Gaps
- No per-sample SDs from the USDA Choice/Select beef papers, the USDA pork studies, or the chicken studies were retrieved.
- No chicken breast with-skin vs skinless data were retrieved in this run. No pork data either.
- No breed or feed effects (grass vs grain) on beef fat were retrieved.

## How large are seasonal and wild-vs-farmed differences in fish fat?

### Takeaway
Fat cycles in pelagic fish are large. Norwegian spring-spawning herring is about 21-22% fat in autumn and loses about 10 points over migration and spawning. Mackerel is reported at under 5% in spring and about 28% in autumn, but that figure is unverified. Farmed Atlantic salmon fillets typically carry 2-4× the fat of wild salmon. EPA+DHA per 100 g can be similar between the two, because farmed fat is diluted with vegetable-oil n-6 fats.

### Cited Findings
- **Farmed vs wild salmon, Molversmyr E, Devle HM, Naess-Andresen CF, Ekeberg D (2022), *Food Science & Nutrition* 10(9):3117-3127, DOI 10.1002/fsn3.2911.** n = 3 fish each, Norway, raw muscle.
  - Muscle lipid: farmed 8.97 ± 0.63%, wild 2.14 ± 0.32%.
  - EPA+DHA: about 523 mg/100 g farmed vs about 520 mg/100 g wild.
  - n-3 / n-6 (mg/100 g): farmed 1,234 / 1,281 (n-6:n-3 about 1.0); wild 673 / 37 (about 0.06).
  - Source: [PMC9469852](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9469852)
- Norwegian thesis: farmed Atlantic salmon fillet total lipid 11.4%, about double that of wild fish (search summary) — [UiT Munin](https://munin.uit.no/handle/10037/3475)
- Multi-city US survey: farmed salmon fillets averaged 16.6% fat, wild 6.4%, supermarket fillets 14.4%. This is a secondary write-up and the primary paper was not identified (search summary) — [naturalhealthresearch.org](https://naturalhealthresearch.org/contaminants-in-farm-raised-versus-wild-caught-salmon/)
- Other studies are reported to show smaller differences: Scottish farmed flesh lipid 12.5% (84% higher than wild), while a Quebec study found similar lipid in farmed and wild fish. The differences were attributed to environment, stock and feed (search summary) — [Global Seafood Alliance](https://www.globalseafood.org/advocate/quality-parameters-in-farmed-atlantic-salmon/)
- Farmed salmon season and growth: fillet fat rose from 3-4% to 17-19% over one year, mostly July-November — [Nofima](https://nofima.com/publication/747198/) (from earlier lite notes)
- **Norwegian spring-spawning herring, Slotte A (1996), IMR.** Fat builds up while feeding in the Norwegian Sea (April-August). It is about 21-22% on arrival at the wintering area, falls only slightly over winter, then drops by about 10 percentage points during the spawning migration and spawning. Fillets were fattier than whole fish. Abstract not fetched: the host could not be resolved (search summary) — [IMR Brage record](https://imr.brage.unit.no/imr-xmlui/handle/11250/105591)
- North Sea autumn-spawning herring: muscle fat rose in May-June, plateaued, and fell by September near spawning, with timing consistent between years. Percentages not retrieved; PDF returned 403 (search summary) — [Kenyon et al., ICES J Mar Sci](https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/files/217895614/Kenyon_etal_icesjms_Intra_and_inter_annual_VOR.pdf)
- North Sea herring fillets (Aidos et al. 2002): fat differed significantly by month. Oil PUFA was lowest in January-March (post-spawning starvation) and highest in June-August (search summary) — [AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65dea883b766d82b18fdf54e)
- Atlantic mackerel: under 5% fat in April and about 28% in October, attributed to an IMR study. The source is a promotional page with no paper cited, so treat as unverified (search summary) — [Seafood from Norway](https://fromnorway.com/stories-from-norway/you-get-the-best-possible-seafood/mackerel-wonderfully-oily)
- An older NOAA report gives mackerel fillet oil of about 22-24% in October samples and about 10-20% in November samples (search summary; date and species not confirmed) — [NOAA MFR](https://spo.nmfs.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-content/mfr2322.pdf)
- Icelandic summer mackerel (July-September 2012-2013): lipid content varied by month and fishing area. Values not shown on the landing page — [Romotowska et al. 2015, Matís report](https://matis.is/en/?p=30274); [PDF](https://zenodo.org/records/12517481)

### Inferences
- Fat in fatty pelagic fish varies severalfold over the year, roughly 5 to 25+%. With protein near 18-20%, this moves energy from about 100 to over 300 kcal/100 g. This is my estimate from fat × 9 kcal/g, not a sourced value.
- Moving from wild to farmed salmon changes total fat and the n-6 share much more than EPA+DHA per 100 g.

### Gaps
- No primary herring fat percentages by month were retrieved; the Slotte abstract and the Kenyon PDF were not readable. No Norwegian IMR Seafood Data table was retrieved.
- No tuna data, and no fillet-position (belly vs loin vs tail) numbers.

## How variable is retail/bulk milk compared with individual cows, and how variable are eggs?

### Takeaway
Between individual cows, fat is the most variable milk component: a CV of about 13% for fat vs about 5% for protein in a Polish Holstein population, and about 28% for fat in a mixed-breed Irish trial. Bulk (herd) milk averages out the differences between cows, but day-to-day fat in herd milk still varies. Breed shifts mean fat by about 1.3-2.5 percentage points. For eggs, hen age moves the yolk:albumen ratio and hence fat per 100 g. No retrieved study gives a CV for proximates in retail eggs.

### Cited Findings
- Polish Holstein population: CV 13% for fat and 5% for protein. In healthy cows on balanced rations, CVs within production groups fell to 7% (fat) and 3% (protein). The PDF could not be text-extracted, so the citation details are unverified (search summary) — [RNZ 2023, 50(2), art. 06](https://rnz.iz.edu.pl/files/RNZ_2023_50_2_art06_en.pdf)
- Irish trial, 60 cows (Jersey, elite and national-average Holstein-Friesian): 4.72 ± 1.30% fat (CV about 28%) and 3.85 ± 0.61% protein (CV about 16%) — [Teagasc](https://t-stor.teagasc.ie/entities/publication/57fa2a2f-8cb4-44a1-9df5-2728f39d8442) (from earlier lite notes)
- Breed: Jersey 5.20% fat / 3.83% protein vs Holstein 3.91% / 3.12% — [UVM](https://scholarworks.uvm.edu/items/867970df-8fed-467e-ba2f-39f1980b9461). In Swedish bulk milk, Jersey fat was 2.2-2.5 points higher than other breeds and season had limited effect — [SLU thesis](https://stud.epsilon.slu.se/15823/) (both from earlier lite notes)
- Within cow: protein % is highly consistent across milkings on a test day, while fat % varies notably (intraclass correlation analysis) (search summary) — [JDS Communications 2025](https://doaj.org/article/41943a4435b047859ca4f18842a81edf)
- Milk yield and fat concentration vary more than protein and lactose, both between animals and between days (search summary) — [Animal Science 1992](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/animal-science/article/sources-of-variation-in-yields-and-concentrations-of-milk-solids-in-dairy-cows/25F95CF7AB5D478405A940A96643F48F)
- Herd bulk milk, 28 UK herds over 13 days: day-to-day fat % variation was "rather greater" than previously thought, and a single bulk sample says little about a herd's milk over several weeks (search summary) — [Edwards, SRUC](https://pure.sruc.ac.uk/en/publications/a-study-of-the-variation-in-the-fat-content-of-herd-bulk-milks/)
- Eggs, hen age:
  - Yolk weight and yolk % rise with age; albumen % and albumen solids fall (Leghorn strains, *Poultry Science* 1983) (search summary) — [Poultry Science 62(6):925](https://academic.oup.com/ps/article/62/6/925/1509525)
  - Yolk:albumen ratio rises with age in commercial layers (search summary) — [Thai Agritech journal](https://li01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/agritechjournal/article/download/271416/182821/1178883)
  - Meat-type hens lay heavier eggs with higher yolk share than egg-type hens (search summary) — [Uni-MATE journal](https://journal.uni-mate.hu/index.php/aak/article/view/1966)
- Eggs, USDA 2010 retail resampling (NFNAP, 9 locations, March/April 2010):
  - Cholesterol fell from 423 to 372 mg/100 g vs 2000-2001, with a 2010 range of 344-405.
  - Vitamin D3 averaged 2.05 µg/100 g (range 0.97-12.1), attributed partly to feed modification.
  - Proximates were reported as comparable to SR22, but the values were not retrieved.
  - Sources (search summary): [USDA ARS egg report](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400525/articles/eb11_egg.pdf); [JFCA paper record](https://agris.fao.org/search/ru/records/65e00178b766d82b1803ec0e)

### Inferences
- Pooling milk from many cows should shrink fat CV roughly by √(number of cows) for independent variation between cows. Breed mix, season and feed remain as systematic effects in retail milk. Standardised retail milk (e.g. 3.5% or 1.5% fat) has fat fixed by specification, so its variability comes mainly from protein and other solids. This is inferred and not sourced here.
- Whole-egg fat per 100 g should rise with hen age as yolk share rises, since yolk carries almost all the fat. No retrieved study quantifies this per 100 g.

### Gaps
- No retail pasteurised milk survey with SDs was retrieved. No lactation-stage or season numbers beyond the qualitative notes.
- No cheese or yoghurt between-brand variability data.
- No numbers per 100 g of egg for fat, protein or energy by hen age, diet or egg size, and no USDA 2010 proximate SDs.

## Which causes dominate per food group, with numbers?

### Takeaway
- **Meat:** cut and trim dominate. Lean lipid ranges 6.5-12.7% raw across Prime cuts, and separable fat is 4-28% of cut weight. Grade is secondary but matters for "lean" status.
- **Fatty fish:** season or spawning stage, and farmed vs wild. Fat changes about 10-20+ points over the cycle; farmed salmon has 2-4× wild fat.
- **Milk:** breed, then individual cow. Breed shifts fat about 1.3-2.5 points; fat CV between individual cows is 13-28%, protein CV 5-16%.
- **Eggs:** hen age and genotype, through yolk share. Not quantified per 100 g.
- **All groups:** protein is the most stable macronutrient, and energy variability tracks fat.

### Cited Findings
- Beef cut: lean lipid 6.5-12.7% and energy 151-202 kcal/100 g raw across cuts of one grade. Separable fat ranges from 3.6% (tenderloin, external + seam) to 27.9% (rib roast) of cut weight — [Mortensen et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11397233/)
- Salmon production system: muscle fat 8.97% farmed vs 2.14% wild, with EPA+DHA unchanged — [Molversmyr et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9469852)
- Herring spawning cycle: about 21-22% fat falls by about 10 points through spawning — [Slotte 1996, IMR](https://imr.brage.unit.no/imr-xmlui/handle/11250/105591)
- Milk: fat CV 13% vs protein CV 5% between cows — [RNZ 2023](https://rnz.iz.edu.pl/files/RNZ_2023_50_2_art06_en.pdf); breed effect — [UVM](https://scholarworks.uvm.edu/items/867970df-8fed-467e-ba2f-39f1980b9461)

### Inferences
- Across animal foods, protein per 100 g of lean tissue sits in a narrow band (beef lean 21.7-23.2% raw). Fat and water trade off against each other: in the beef data, moisture falls as lipid rises. So a fat measurement or estimate predicts most of the energy variation.

### Gaps
- Pork, lamb, chicken (skin on vs off), tuna, cheese and yoghurt were not covered by retrieved sources.
- Breed and feed effects in meat, and diet effects on egg fatty acids, are not quantified here.

# Labels, brands and food composition databases: how well they represent the food actually eaten

Method note: the abstracts and metadata were retrieved from the Europe PMC REST API (PubMed web pages were blocked by a cookie wall). Full text was read for Urban 2010 and Jumpertz 2013 (PMC), 21 CFR 101.9 (eCFR API), and the EU December 2012 tolerance guidance (the PDF text was extracted by hand). DOIs and PMIDs are given for each paper. Figures that were only seen in press or blog coverage are marked as such.

## 1. Primary label-accuracy studies (declared vs measured)

### Takeaway
For US packaged foods, measured energy is on average a few percent **above** the label: median +4.3% for snacks and mean +8% for frozen meals. The spread for individual products is wide, and the macronutrient errors are large: carbohydrate up to +157% and fat from −59% to +87%. Restaurant foods are accurate on average (+10 kcal/portion) but very inaccurate for individual items: 19% of items were ≥100 kcal above their stated value, and low-calorie items were systematically understated. Surveillance in other countries finds that 12–18% of products (energy, sodium, saturated fat, sugar) fall outside ±20%, rising to most products in small studies. Errors on "nutrients to limit" (energy, sodium) lean towards the food containing more than stated. Fortified foods and supplements are often well above or below label: infant formula reached up to 419%, and vitamin D pills ranged from 9% to 146%.

### Cited Findings
**Jumpertz et al. 2013** (Jumpertz R, Venti CA, Le DS, Michaels J, Parrington S, Krakoff J, Votruba S. "Food label accuracy of common snack foods." *Obesity* 21(1):164–169; doi:10.1002/oby.20185; PMID 23505182; PMC3605747)
- n = 24 popular US energy-dense snack products (candy bars, chips, cereal bars/pastries, cookies, crackers, ice cream, nuts, yoghurt). Energy was measured by bomb calorimetry in April–May 2010. Macronutrients were measured in a convenience subsample of 10 items (Sep–Oct 2010) by AOAC methods: fat by acid hydrolysis, protein as N×6.25, carbohydrate by difference. — [Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23505182); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3605747/)
- Serving weight exceeded the label by a median 1.2% (IQR −1.4 to 4.3; P = 0.10). After adjusting for serving size, metabolisable energy was **+6.8 kcal per serving (IQR 0.5 to 23.5; P = 0.0003), or +4.3% (IQR 0.2 to 13.7; P = 0.001)** above the label. — [Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23505182)
- In the 10-item subsample, carbohydrate was +7.7% (IQR 0.8 to 16.7; P = 0.01). Fat (−12.8% [−38.6, 9.6]) and protein (+6.1% [−6.1, 17.5]) did not differ significantly from the label. Carbohydrate explained 40% of the excess calories and serving size a further 55%. The authors conclude the products are "well within FDA limits". — [Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23505182)
- Item level (Table 1, 10 items): carbohydrate deviation ranged from −1.6% (Kellogg's Pop-Tarts) to +157.1% (Yoplait Smoothie). Fat deviation ranged from −58.9% (Chips Ahoy) to +87.1% (Yoplait Smoothie). Item-level energy deviations appear only in a figure and are not given in the text. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3605747/)

**Urban et al. 2010** (Urban LE, Dallal GE, Robinson LM, Ausman LM, Saltzman E, Roberts SB. "The accuracy of stated energy contents of reduced-energy, commercially prepared foods." *J Am Diet Assoc* 110(1):116–123; doi:10.1016/j.jada.2009.10.003; PMID 20102837; PMC2838242)
- **29 restaurant foods** (quick-serve and sit-down): stated 303 ± 123 kcal, measured 329 ± 145 kcal, mean difference **+18% (SD 59%)**. **10 supermarket frozen meals**: stated 248 ± 75 kcal, measured 264 ± 78 kcal, mean difference **+8% (SD 14%)**. Neither difference was significant (P = 0.12 each), because of the high variability. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2838242/); [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/20102837)
- Item range: restaurant items ran from −36% (toast) to **+200%** (grits; 86 kcal stated vs 258 kcal measured). Frozen meals ran from −10% to +31%. Free side dishes added 471 ± 167 kcal, bringing provided energy to an average 245% of the entrée's stated value. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2838242/)
- Method: freeze-dried samples analysed in duplicate by isoperibol bomb calorimetry, with stated metabolisable energy converted to gross energy using USDA factors. Test meals measured −1.9% ± 0.3% of their calculated values, which the authors use as the method's accuracy and precision. Portion-weight discrepancy significantly predicted the energy discrepancy (P < 0.0001). Pilot study, one unit per food. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2838242/)

**Urban et al. 2011** (Urban LE, McCrory MA, Dallal GE, Das SK, Saltzman E, Weber JL, Roberts SB. "Accuracy of stated energy contents of restaurant foods." *JAMA* 306(3):287–293; doi:10.1001/jama.2011.993; PMID 21771989; PMC4363942)
- 42 randomly selected quick-serve and sit-down restaurants (Massachusetts, Arkansas, Indiana; Jan–Jun 2010), **269 items / 242 unique foods**, measured by bomb calorimetry corrected with standard metabolisable-energy factors. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21771989)
- Overall stated vs measured difference was **+10 kcal/portion (95% CI −15 to 34; P = 0.52)**, so restaurant labels are accurate on average. However, **50 of 269 items (19%) measured ≥100 kcal/portion above the stated value**. The top 10% of over-runners (13 of 17 resampled) were +289 kcal/portion (95% CI 186–392) at first sampling and +258 kcal (154–361) at second sampling. Foods with low stated energy were understated and foods with high stated energy were overstated (P < 0.001). — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21771989)

**USDA Nutrient Data Laboratory, US sodium** (Ahuja JKC et al. 2019. "Comparison of Label and Laboratory Sodium Values in Popular Sodium-Contributing Foods in the United States." *J Acad Nutr Diet* 119(2); doi:10.1016/j.jand.2018.08.155; PMID 30446429)
- 3,432 samples of 125 foods were collected in 2010–2014, and 1,390 composites of 114 foods were linked to label or restaurant-website values. For sodium, **5% of composites had a lab/label ratio >120% and 14% had a ratio ≤80%**. Ratios ≤80% were found in 22% of private-label composites vs 12% of national brands, and in 37% of sit-down restaurant composites vs 9% of fast-food composites. Ratios >120% were found in 3% of store composites vs 11% of restaurant composites. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30446429)

**Canada** (Fitzpatrick L, Arcand J, L'Abbé M, et al. 2014. "Accuracy of Canadian food labels for sodium content of food." *Nutrients* 6(8):3326; doi:10.3390/nu6083326; PMID 25153971)
- More than 1,000 CFIA samples (2006–2010). **16.7% (n = 169) were "unsatisfactory" (beyond ±20% of the label)**: sodium 18.4%, saturated fat 15.8%, calories 14.2%, sugar 12.9%, trans fat 4.3%. **All unsatisfactory products had more of the nutrient than declared.** Sodium and calories were consistently under-reported (p < 0.05). — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25153971)
- Potassium in Canada (Picard K et al. 2025. *Nutrients* 17(18):2935; doi:10.3390/nu17182935): 376 CFIA products with paired values. The mean analysed-minus-label difference was +15 mg/serving (SD 68). 69.7% differed from the label by >±10%, with 48.1% under-reporting and 23.9% over-reporting; κ = 0.376. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41010461)

**Spain** (Yusta-Boyo MJ et al. 2020. "Sugar Content in Processed Foods in Spain and a Comparison of Mandatory Nutrition Labelling and Laboratory Values." *Nutrients* 12(4):1078; doi:10.3390/nu12041078; PMID 32295050)
- 1,173 of the most commonly consumed processed foods (28 groups, 77 subcategories). **98.4% met the EU sugar tolerance ranges**. Only one subcategory (cured ham) showed a significant gap between label and analysed medians. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32295050)

**Italy** (Lo Turco V et al. 2023. "Assessment of the Accuracy of Nutrition Label and Chemical Composition of Plant-Based Milks Available on the Italian Market." *Foods* 12(17):3207; doi:10.3390/foods12173207)
- "Most labels provided inaccurate information, especially with respect to the declared energy, fat, and saturated fat." The abstract gives no percentages. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/37685140)

**Australia** (Fabiansson SU. 2006. "Precision in nutritional information declarations on food labels in Australia." *Asia Pac J Clin Nutr* 15(4); PMID 17077059)
- 350 samples of 70 products. The average deviation for individual nutrient components ranged from **−13% to +61%**. Only 16% of products would fully comply with a symmetric ±20% rule on every nutrient, rising to 51% with asymmetric limits (−20% for beneficial nutrients, +20% for unfavourable ones). Excluding trivial amounts raised these to 27% and 70%. Australia had no legal tolerance at the time. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17077059)

**Brazil**
- Lobanco CM et al. 2009 (*Rev Saúde Pública* 43(3); doi:10.1590/s0034-89102009000300014): 153 industrial foods aimed at children and adolescents (São Paulo, 2001–2005), judged against the legal ±20% tolerance. **All savoury products failed on fibre, sodium and saturated fat. 52% of cookies failed on saturated fat.** — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19347174)
- Bernardo GL et al. 2021 (*Public Health Nutr*; doi:10.1017/s1368980020000786): bakery bread rolls. Label sodium was 478 ± 93 mg/100 g vs **618 ± 74 mg/100 g by lab analysis (P < 0.001)**, about 29% under-declared. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32618554)

**Other national checks (sodium/salt)**
- Bangladesh (Afroza U et al. 2025, *Foods* 14(21):3587; doi:10.3390/foods14213587): 105 processed and ultra-processed foods. **40% under-reported sodium** beyond ±20%, and 9% had no sodium label. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41227565)
- Iran (Shirani F et al. 2024, *J Res Health Sci*; doi:10.34172/jrhs.2024.151): 53 packaged nuts and seeds. **82% had salt values that disagreed with the traffic-light label.** — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/39072552)
- Hospital nutritional fluids, Australia (Hopper Z et al. 2025, *Br J Nutr*; doi:10.1017/s0007114525000601): 69 fluids, with equivalence tested at ±10% by bomb calorimetry. 14 (20%) were non-equivalent, and 11 of these 14 contained *less* energy than calculated. Prescribed medical fluids were 34/35 equivalent, but local recipes were only 2/11. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/40123449)

**Fortified foods and supplements**
- Holick MF et al. 1992 (*N Engl J Med* 326:1178; doi:10.1056/nejm199204303261802): 42 samples of 13 US milk brands and 10 samples of 5 infant formula brands. Only **29% of milk samples** contained 80–120% of the labelled vitamin D, and 62% contained <80%. No vitamin D was detected in 3 of 14 skim samples. **7 of 10 formula samples had >200% of the label (maximum 419%), and none had less than the label.** — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/1313548)
- Alvi SN et al. 2016 (*Ann Saudi Med*; doi:10.5144/0256-4947.2016.325): 125 samples of fortified low-fat milk labelled 400 IU/L. Mean 10.2 (SD 1.6) ng/mL against a labelled 10 ng/mL (range 7.1–13.9). 20% fell outside ±20% (10.4% under, 9.6% over), and inter-producer CV was 16.1%. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27710983)
- Andrews KW et al. 2018 (Dietary Supplement Ingredient Database, *J Nutr*; doi:10.1093/jn/nxy092): "many ingredients are added at higher than declared label amounts, but overages are not standardized." **Overages >20% of label are predicted for several nutrients in 50–99% of NHANES multivitamin-mineral products**: iodine and selenium in adult products; iodine, vitamin D and vitamin E in children's products; iodine, chromium and potassium in prenatal products. Calcium overages are 10–20% in most products, and folic acid >10% in the vast majority. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31505677)
- LeBlanc ES et al. 2013 ("Over-the-counter and compounded vitamin D: is potency what we expect?" *JAMA Intern Med* 173(7):585; doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2013.3812): a research letter with no abstract in Europe PMC. Press coverage reports 55 OTC bottles from 12 manufacturers plus compounded pills, with **potency from 9% to 146% of label**, and pills within one bottle ranging from 52% to 135%. Coverage disagrees on whether the top of the range is 140% or 146%. — [Europe PMC record](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23400578); [NutraIngredients](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2013/02/11/Vitamin-D-content-of-supplements-varies-widely-says-new-analysis/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/02/130211162231.htm) (secondary)

### Inferences
- Packaged foods under regulatory oversight (US FDA, EU) cluster within a few percent of the label for energy (Jumpertz +4.3%; Spain 98.4% compliant for sugar). Average bias is small, but a minority of items are far off, especially for individual macronutrients.
- Restaurant and artisanal foods (bakery bread, sit-down restaurants, local hospital recipes) have much larger item-level errors in both directions. Urban 2011's "low-energy items understated, high-energy items overstated" pattern is consistent with regression to the mean, or with portion control that matters more for smaller items.
- Where errors go beyond the tolerance for energy or sodium, they lean towards the food containing more than declared (Canada: all unsatisfactory products had excess; Urban 2010; Brazilian bread). The nutrients consumers want more of (vitamins, minerals in fortified products) lean towards overage. Both directions are what the legal tolerances reward (Section 2).

### Gaps
- The figure "96% of items within 20% of label", attributed to Jumpertz in a secondary review (see the earlier lite note), **could not be verified in the paper's text**. Item-level energy deviations appear only in a figure, so do not quote it.
- No Irish (FSAI) or UK (FSA or local trading standards) label-accuracy surveillance with numbers was retrieved. A large Italian analytical survey of packaged foods (beyond plant milks) was also not found.
- Lo Turco 2023 percentages were not extracted from the full text.
- The LeBlanc 2013 numbers come only from press coverage. The primary letter was not accessible (jamanetwork returned 404).

## 2. Legal tolerances and how label values are derived

### Takeaway
**US (21 CFR 101.9(g))**: compliance is tested on a composite of 12 consumer units. Calories, sugars, total, saturated and trans fat, cholesterol and sodium are misbranded only if they are **>120% of the label**; being under the label is "reasonable" under current good manufacturing practice. Naturally occurring protein, carbohydrate, fibre, vitamins and minerals must be **≥80%**. Added (fortified) nutrients must be **≥100%**, and "reasonable excesses" are acceptable. **EU (December 2012 Commission guidance)**: tolerances are symmetric. Carbohydrate, sugars, protein and fibre are ±2 g (<10 g/100 g), ±20% (10–40 g) or ±8 g (>40 g). Fat is ±1.5 g, ±20% or ±8 g. Saturates are ±0.8 g (<4 g) or ±20%. Sodium is ±0.15 g (<0.5 g) or ±20%, and salt ±0.375 g (<1.25 g) or ±20%. Vitamins are +50%/−35% and minerals +45%/−35% (supplements −20%). Measurement uncertainty is included, and **energy has no tolerance of its own**. Both regimes allow labels derived by calculation from databases or recipes instead of analysis.

### Cited Findings
- 21 CFR 101.9(g)(2): "The sample for nutrient analysis shall consist of a composite of 12 subsamples (consumer units), taken 1 from each of 12 different randomly chosen shipping cases", analysed by AOAC methods. — [eCFR 21 CFR 101.9](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.9)
- 101.9(g)(3)–(4): Class I nutrients are those added in fortified or fabricated foods, and "must be formulated to be at least equal to the value ... declared". Class II (naturally occurring) vitamins, minerals, protein, total carbohydrate, PUFA, MUFA and fibre must be "at least equal to 80 percent" of the label. If an exogenous source is added, the whole amount is Class I. — [eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.9)
- 101.9(g)(5)–(6): calories, total sugars, added sugars, total, saturated and trans fat, cholesterol and sodium are misbranded if "greater than 20 percent in excess" of the label. "Reasonable excesses" of vitamins, minerals, protein, carbohydrate and fibre, and "reasonable deficiencies" of calories, sugars, fat and sodium, "are acceptable within current good manufacturing practice". No regulatory action is taken for deviations smaller than the method's recognised analytical variability. — [eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.9)
- 101.9(g)(8): as an alternative, compliance can be shown with an FDA-approved database computed under FDA guideline procedures (e.g. raw produce, seafood). — [eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.9)
- 101.9(c)(1)(i): six permitted calorie methods. (A) specific Atwater factors (USDA Handbook 74, table 13); (B) general 4/4/9 on total carbohydrate; (C) 4/4/9 on carbohydrate minus non-digestible carbohydrate and sugar alcohols, plus 2 kcal/g for soluble non-digestible carbohydrate; (D) FDA-approved specific food factors; (E) bomb calorimetry minus 1.25 kcal per g protein; (F) specified sugar-alcohol factors. Calories are rounded to 5 kcal up to 50 kcal and to 10 kcal above. — [eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.9)
- EU guidance, "Guidance document for competent authorities for the control of compliance with EU legislation ... with regard to the setting of tolerances for nutrient values declared on a label" (European Commission, DG SANCO, **December 2012**). It states: "This Document has no formal legal status". Table 1 (foods other than supplements, uncertainty included) is as summarised in the Takeaway, with a note that higher upper tolerances may be accepted for vitamin C in liquids. Energy is not listed in Table 1. — [EC guidance PDF](https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2016-10/labelling_nutrition-vitamins_minerals-guidance_tolerances_1212_en.pdf)
- EU guidance, Table 2 (food supplements): vitamins +50%/−20%, minerals +45%/−20%. For nutrients with claims and for added vitamins and minerals (section 5), only measurement uncertainty is allowed on the "misleading" side (below the declared level for vitamins and minerals), while wider deviations are accepted above. EU maximum amounts cap the upper tolerance. — [EC guidance PDF](https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2016-10/labelling_nutrition-vitamins_minerals-guidance_tolerances_1212_en.pdf)
- EU guidance, section 2.2: "The measured value should be within the tolerances around the declared value during the entire shelf life." This is why fortificants are dosed high at production. — [EC guidance PDF](https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2016-10/labelling_nutrition-vitamins_minerals-guidance_tolerances_1212_en.pdf)
- EU guidance, sections 1–2: under Regulation 1169/2011, declared values are "average values" based on "a) the manufacturer's analysis of the food; b) a calculation from the known or actual average values of the ingredients used; c) a calculation from generally established and accepted data". Deviations arise from "the source of values (values derived from literature and calculated by recipe instead of analysis), the accuracy of analysis, the variation in the raw materials, the effect of processing, nutrient stability and storage". — [EC guidance PDF](https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2016-10/labelling_nutrition-vitamins_minerals-guidance_tolerances_1212_en.pdf)
- EU guidance, section 2.3 (anti-gaming clause): declared values "should not be established at either extreme of a defined tolerance range". For fats, sugars and salt, the declared value should not sit at the lower tolerance while the true average is higher. — [EC guidance PDF](https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2016-10/labelling_nutrition-vitamins_minerals-guidance_tolerances_1212_en.pdf)
- EU guidance, section 6 (rounding): tolerances are applied to the rounding bounds. A declared 12 g of protein means 11.5–12.4 g, giving an upper limit of 12.4 × 1.2 = 14.88, which rounds to 15 g. Energy is declared to the nearest 1 kJ/kcal. Fat, carbohydrate and similar nutrients at ≤0.5 g/100 g may be declared as 0 or "<0.5 g". — [EC guidance PDF](https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2016-10/labelling_nutrition-vitamins_minerals-guidance_tolerances_1212_en.pdf)
- FDA states that using databases to derive label values is voluntary, and does not prescribe how label values are generated. — [FDA database guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/guidance-industry-guide-developing-and-using-data-bases-nutrition-labeling) (from the earlier lite note; not re-fetched)

### Inferences
- The US rules are one-sided. A manufacturer is safe declaring *more* than it contains for calories, fat, sugar and sodium (under-delivery is a "reasonable deficiency"), but at risk if the food contains >120%. The reverse holds for protein, fibre and vitamins. The incentive is to declare energy and sodium near or above the expected mean and to over-fortify vitamins. Measured results nonetheless sit slightly above the label for energy (Jumpertz +4.3%; Urban +8% for frozen meals), within the 20% headroom. A plausible reading is that labels are often calculated, and the 20% window absorbs batch variation and serving-weight overfill.
- The EU regime is symmetric and works in grams at low concentrations. For a low-sugar food at 3 g/100 g, ±2 g is ±67% in relative terms. Relative tolerances are effectively much wider for minor components.
- Because the EU sets no energy tolerance, energy compliance follows indirectly from the macronutrient tolerances. In the worst case, carbohydrate +20%, fat +20% and protein +20% could stack to give energy about 20% above the label while each macronutrient is still "compliant".
- Shelf-life compliance plus a narrow lower tolerance for added vitamins (EU) or the ≥100% rule (US) structurally produces overages. This matches the DSID finding of >20% overages in many supplements.

### Gaps
- The share of labels derived by calculation versus analysis in industry practice was not quantified by any source found.
- The EU Regulation 1169/2011 Annex XIV energy conversion factors (e.g. fibre 2 kcal/g, polyols 2.4 kcal/g) could not be fetched (EUR-Lex and legislation.gov.uk returned empty pages). The figures quoted here are from memory and not verified in this run.
- Codex, Australian/New Zealand (FSANZ) and Brazilian (ANVISA) tolerance rules were not retrieved directly. Brazil's ±20% rule is cited only via Lobanco 2009, and Australia's lack of a legal tolerance in 2006 only via Fabiansson 2006.

## 3. Brand-to-brand variability for the same food

### Takeaway
Within one generic category, declared composition differs between brands by much more than label error does. UK sliced bread salt has a CV of about 13% (0.98 ± 0.13 g/100 g). UK yoghurt sugar ranges from 0.1 to 21 g/100 g within categories. The same fast-food item varies about 2.7-fold in salt and 1.8-fold in fat between countries. Vitamin D in fortified milk varies 16% between producers. The choice of brand is therefore usually a larger source of error than label inaccuracy.

### Cited Findings
- UK bread (Brinsden HC et al. 2013, *BMJ Open* 3:e002936; doi:10.1136/bmjopen-2013-002936): salt was 1.23 ± 0.19 g/100 g in 2001 (40 products), 1.05 ± 0.16 in 2006 (138) and **0.98 ± 0.13 in 2011 (203)**. In 2011 own-brand bread averaged 0.95 g/100 g vs 1.04 for branded bread. The authors note "a wide variation in salt levels". — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23794567)
- UK yoghurts (Moore JB, Horti A, Fielding BA 2018, *BMJ Open* 8:e021387; doi:10.1136/bmjopen-2017-021387): 921 products. Median total sugar (range) was 10.8 g/100 g (4.8–14.5) for children's, **11.9 (4.6–21.3) for fruit**, 12.0 (0.1–18.8) for flavoured, 13.1 (3.8–16.9) for organic and 5.0 (1.6–9.5) for natural/Greek. "The ranges were extremely broad." — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30228100)
- UK yoghurt follow-up (Moore JB et al. 2020, *Nutrients* 12(1):171; doi:10.3390/nu12010171): 893 products. Median sugar fell from 11.9 (IQR 8.8–13.6) to 10.4 g/100 g (6.6–13.0) between 2016 and 2019. **40% of 2019 products were new**, and only 32% of matched products were reformulated (mean −0.65 g/100 g). — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31936185)
- Fast food across countries (Dunford E et al. 2012, *CMAJ* 184(9):1023; doi:10.1503/cmaj.111895): McDonald's Chicken McNuggets had **0.6 g salt/100 g in the UK vs 1.6 g in the US**. Chicken products averaged 1.1 g in the UK vs 1.8 g in the US. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22508978)
- Fast food energy and fat across 10 countries (Ziauddeen N et al. 2015, *Public Health Nutr*; doi:10.1017/s1368980015000336): 2,961 products. Chicken dishes ranged from 649 to 1,197 kJ/100 g between countries and sandwiches from 552 to 1,050 kJ/100 g. Chicken McNuggets had **12 g fat/100 g in Germany vs 21.1 g in New Zealand**. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25702788)
- US sodium, branded label database vs national reference (Maalouf J et al. 2015, *Procedia Food Sci*; doi:10.1016/j.profoo.2015.06.016): 937 products across 23 Sentinel Foods, with 3 to 126 products per food (126 for white bread). Mean sodium for 17 of 23 foods (74%) was within 90–110% of USDA SR26, and 6 foods differed significantly. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26484010)
- Vitamin D milk (Alvi 2016, see Section 1): inter-producer CV 16.1% and inter-batch CV 8.2–20.8%. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27710983)
- Italian plant-based milks (Lo Turco 2023): a PCA "confirmed that nutritional standards cannot be unequivocally established for a given PBM". — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/37685140)

### Inferences
- When a user logs a generic food ("yoghurt", "bread") instead of the specific brand, the expected error for sugar or salt is roughly the within-category spread. For fruit yoghurt that is several g/100 g (IQR about ±2–3 g), which exceeds typical label error.
- Products turn over quickly (40% new in 3 years for UK yoghurts), so database entries for branded foods go stale.

### Gaps
- No quantified brand-spread studies were retrieved for **breakfast cereals, peanut butter or chocolate**, and none for macronutrient or energy spread (as opposed to salt or sugar) in bread. Branded-food databases such as USDA Branded Foods, Open Food Facts or FoodSwitch could be analysed directly to fill this gap.

## 4. Disagreement between food composition databases (including crowdsourced and app databases) and its causes

### Takeaway
Reference national databases mostly agree on energy and macronutrients. The disagreements are mainly about **definitions and methods**: fibre method, carbohydrate by difference vs available, energy factors, folate, vitamin A/E expression, and data age. EPIC concluded that nine European tables were "not comparable" for several nutrients without standardisation. Including fibre energy alone changes energy by ≥10% for 321 of 1,753 Brazilian foods, though by <3% for whole menus. App and crowdsourced databases correlate well with reference databases for energy at the diet level (r ≈ 0.9–0.96 for MyFitnessPal vs Belgian Nubel). They are worse for fibre, sodium, cholesterol and micronutrients, and for individual entries (ICC 0.33–0.43 for fruits in MyFitnessPal). No peer-reviewed quantitative audit of Open Food Facts entry accuracy was found.

### Cited Findings
**Reference databases**
- Deharveng G, Charrondière UR, Slimani N, Southgate DA, Riboli E. 1999 (*Eur J Clin Nutr* 53(1):60–79; doi:10.1038/sj.ejcn.1600677): compared the tables of Denmark, France, Germany, Greece, Great Britain, Italy, the Netherlands, Spain and Sweden. Most nutrients are compatible, but **folate and dietary fibre** lack common methods and definitions, and **energy, protein, carbohydrates, carotenes, vitamins A and E** lack a common mode of expression, "so values are not comparable". Vitamin C values vary widely because of natural variation. Compiled tables mix sources, and some values are more than 20 years old with outdated methods. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/10048800)
- Slimani N et al. 2007 (EPIC Nutrient Database, *Eur J Clin Nutr* 61(9):1037; doi:10.1038/sj.ejcn.1602679): 550–1,500 foods per country were matched across 10 national databases and standardised for 26 components by unit, mode of expression, definition and method, because "there is currently no European reference NDB". — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17375121)
- Charrondière UR, Vignat J, Riboli E. 2002 (*IARC Sci Publ* 156): "Differences in calculating fibre intake of a British diet when applying the British, Danish and French food composition tables". Title only; no abstract was available. — [record](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12484119)
- Menezes EW et al. 2016 (*Food Chem* 193:128; doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2015.01.051): recalculated the energy of 1,753 Brazilian foods with and without fibre energy. **≥10% energy difference in 321 foods** (mainly vegetables, legumes and fruits), but **<3% for typical mixed daily menus**. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26433298)
- Kim ES et al. 2003 (*Tohoku J Exp Med* 200:7; doi:10.1620/tjem.200.7): Korean food-table estimates vs chemical analysis of 66 duplicate diets. Energy agreed to within 2% (estimated/measured >98%) and protein was 101%, but **carbohydrate was −8% and fat +24%**. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12862306)
- Maalouf 2015 (Section 3): 74% of US branded-label sodium means were within ±10% of USDA SR26. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26484010)
- Merchant AT, Dehghan M. 2006 (*Nutr J* 5:2; doi:10.1186/1475-2891-5-2): the choice of food composition table "is also a source of errors". The PURE study built a USDA-based multi-country database adjusted with local tables to reduce differential error between countries. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16423289)

**App and crowdsourced databases**
- Evenepoel C et al. 2020 (*J Med Internet Res* 22(10):e18237; doi:10.2196/18237): 50 participants, 4-day records, MyFitnessPal vs the Belgian Nubel database. Implausible entries required cleaning limits per portion (e.g. 1,500 kcal or 95 g carbohydrate), and 2.8% of entries were rejected. Cleaned values correlated with Nubel at r = 0.96 for energy, 0.90 for carbohydrate, fat and protein, 0.80 for fibre and 0.79 for sugar, but only **ρ = 0.51 for cholesterol and 0.53 for sodium**. There was a fixed bias for fibre and a proportional bias for cholesterol, and a 5–10% loss of statistical power. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/33084583)
- Fallaize R et al. 2019 (*JMIR mHealth uHealth* 7(2):e9838; doi:10.2196/mhealth.9838): 20 weighed 24-hour records analysed in 5 apps vs Dietplan6 (UK reference). Energy and saturated fat did not differ significantly. Lose It! and FatSecret gave significantly lower protein and sodium. MyFitnessPal and Samsung Health underestimated calcium, iron and vitamin C. Correlations ranged from r = −0.12 (iron) to 0.91 (protein). — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30785409)
- Lin A et al. 2020 (*Curr Dev Nutr* 4(Suppl 2), conference abstract; PMC7258431): 50 most-consumed foods, MyFitnessPal and CalorieKing vs NDSR. CalorieKing had ICC 0.90–1.00 for everything. MyFitnessPal had ICC ≥0.90 except fat (0.89) and **fibre (0.67)**, and within fruits **ICC 0.33–0.43 for calories, carbohydrate and fibre**. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC7258431)
- Morello O et al. 2025 (*J Hum Nutr Diet*; doi:10.1111/jhn.70148): 43 three-day records from Canadian endurance athletes. MyFitnessPal had poor validity vs the Canadian Nutrient File for energy, carbohydrate, protein, cholesterol, sugar and fibre, attributed to "copious options for each food entry ... including non-verified consumer entries". Cronometer showed good to excellent agreement. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41133373)
- Banal MG et al. 2024 (*BMJ Nutr Prev Health*; doi:10.1136/bmjnph-2023-000770): MyFitnessPal vs Philippine food composition tables in 37 adults. MyFitnessPal underestimated energy, carbohydrate and fat and overestimated protein, with very weak to moderate Bland–Altman agreement. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/38966115)
- Teixeira V et al. 2018 (*Nutr Diet* 75(2):219; doi:10.1111/1747-0080.12401): 30 students, MyFitnessPal vs Brazilian tables. MyFitnessPal tended to underestimate, which the authors attribute to "inadequacies in the MFP database". Validity was good for energy and fibre. — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29280547)
- **Open Food Facts**: no peer-reviewed accuracy audit was found. Secondary dataset overviews describe "variable data quality, including missing/malformed fields (such as decimal notation)", and analysts typically drop values above 10× the 99th percentile. — [Emergent Mind overview](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/open-food-facts-dataset) (secondary, low weight)
- Claimed "15–27% error rates" for crowdsourced entries circulate mainly on a competitor vendor's blog (Nutrola), with inconsistent and partly wrong citations. For example, Evenepoel 2020 is cited there as appearing in *Nutrients*, but it was published in JMIR. **Treat these figures as unreliable.** — [Nutrola blog](https://nutrola.app/en/blog/crowdsourced-vs-verified-food-databases-accuracy-study)

**Causes of disagreement (documented)**
- Analytical method and definitional differences for fibre and folate; expression differences for energy, carbohydrate and vitamins A/E; mixed and old sources (Deharveng 1999). — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/10048800)
- Energy calculation choices: US law permits six methods (specific Atwater factors, 4/4/9 on total carbohydrate, 4/4/9 on carbohydrate net of non-digestible carbohydrate plus 2 kcal/g, bomb calorimetry minus 1.25 kcal/g protein, and others). The same food can therefore carry different legal calorie values. — [eCFR 101.9(c)(1)(i)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.9). Counting fibre energy alone shifts ≥10% for about 18% of foods (Menezes 2016). — [abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26433298)
- Real compositional variation (raw material, processing, storage) and recipe-calculated vs analysed values (EC guidance, section 2). — [EC guidance PDF](https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2016-10/labelling_nutrition-vitamins_minerals-guidance_tolerances_1212_en.pdf)
- Crowdsourced specific: duplicate unverified user entries, unit and decimal errors, entries for the wrong preparation state (Evenepoel; Morello; Kraleva 2020 found protein labelling "unclear" in 55% of 462 UK supermarket-website foods because of cooked vs as-sold ambiguity). — [Evenepoel](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/33084583); [Morello](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41133373); [Kraleva 2020, *Nutrients* 12:3440, doi:10.3390/nu12113440](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/33182603)

### Inferences
- For energy at the whole-diet level, reference databases (and cleaned app data) agree to within a few percent. Disagreement grows for single foods, for high-fibre or plant foods (fibre energy, carbohydrate definition), and for micronutrients.
- The order of error sources for a single logged item is roughly: wrong or unverified crowdsourced entry or portion > brand-to-brand spread > definitional differences between databases (for fibre-rich foods) > label error for regulated packaged foods. This ordering is reasoned from the studies above; no single study ranks them.
- Carbohydrate "by difference" (as in Jumpertz's lab analysis and USDA) includes fibre and analytical residue. "Available carbohydrate" tables (UK CoFID style) exclude fibre. The two differ systematically for fibre-rich foods, consistent with the Menezes result.

### Gaps
- No quantitative per-food comparison of **USDA FoodData Central vs UK CoFID, German BLS or Danish Frida** was found. Only qualitative EPIC comparisons (1999) and standardisation work (2007) were found.
- No peer-reviewed error-rate audit of Open Food Facts or MyFitnessPal entries (fraction of entries wrong by >X%) was located. The circulating figures trace back to vendor blogs.
- CoFID, BLS and Frida documentation on carbohydrate expression and energy factors was not fetched in this run.

# Micronutrient variability within the same food

Note: the search tool returned summaries, not full texts. I fetched no primary papers. Figures below come from those summaries and are flagged where unverified.

## 1. What CVs, ranges, fold differences or SDs are reported within a single food?

### Takeaway
Within-food ranges are small for some micronutrients (iodine in milk, roughly 2-fold across seasons and farm types). They are very large for soil-dependent or exposure-dependent ones: selenium in Brazil nuts spans more than 100-fold, and vitamin D2 in mushrooms spans about 800-fold with UV-B. I found no CV % figures from USDA SR Legacy or NFNAP in this run (see Gaps).

### Cited Findings
- Selenium in Brazil nuts, by Amazon state: median 2.07 mg/kg (Mato Grosso) to 68.15 mg/kg (Amazonas), about 33-fold between medians. Soil Se rose with nut Se, and nut Se fell in acidic soils. — [Chemosphere 2017, UNESP repository](https://repositorio.unesp.br/handle/11449/163410?show=full)
- Selenium in individual Brazil nuts, same study (conference abstract): <0.55 to 362.29 mg/kg, mean 37.89 mg/kg. — [Scisoc abstract](https://scisoc.confex.com/crops/2016am/webprogram/Paper100561.html)
- Selenium in Brazil nuts, Embrapa: 0.03-31.7 mg/kg fresh weight in Acre-Rondônia and 1.25-512.0 mg/kg fresh weight in Manaus-Belém. — [Embrapa](https://www.alice.cnptia.embrapa.br/alice/bitstream/doc/1037646/1/CPAFAP2015AreallBrazil.pdf). Both figures came through a search summary and are unchecked.
- Selenium in Brazil nuts, databases: USDA lists 1.92 mg/100 g and UK McCance & Widdowson lists 0.254 mg/100 g, about a 7-fold difference. The cause is probably sampling origin. — [Hodmedods summary](https://hodmedods.co.uk/blogs/news/selenium-in-brazil-nuts-good-but-highly-variable). This is a secondary source.
- Iodine in Swiss UHT milk: organic averaged 71 ± 25 µg/L (SD) and conventional 111 ± 26 µg/L. That is a CV of about 35% for organic and about 23% for conventional (my calculation). Monthly means ran from 42 (organic, Aug-Oct) to 99 µg/L (organic, Jan) and from 75 to 145 µg/L for conventional. Heat treatment had no effect. — [Agroscope](https://ira.agroscope.ch/de-CH/publication/37387)
- Iodine in Swiss farm milk: median 55 µg/L on organic farms and 93 µg/L on conventional farms. By month, 53 µg/L in September, 97 in December and 101 in March. Iodine-containing teat dips also raised levels. — [ETH Zurich](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/247182)
- Iodine in US Northeast retail milk: summer about 356 µg/L and spring about 479 µg/L. Season was significant (P<0.001), but organic (410) versus conventional (425) was not (P=0.45). — [SARE tables](https://projects.sare.org/media/pdf/T/a/b/Tables-11-13.pdf)
- Iodine in milk, systematic review of 34 countries: winter higher than summer by a mean 5.97 µg/100 g, and conventional higher than organic by 6.00 µg/100 g. The organic gap was significant only in summer. — [Surrey/Reading review](https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/permalink/44SUR_INST/15d8lgh/alma99904766502346)
- Vitamin D2 in white button mushrooms (Agaricus bisporus): 0.2 to 164 µg/100 g fresh weight, depending on UV-B dose. Wild and untreated cultivated mushrooms had about 0.2-1.5 µg/100 g. One UV-B regime gave about 33 µg/100 g, and post-harvest UV-B gave about 32 µg/100 g. — [Food & Nutrition Research 2012](https://foodandnutritionresearch.net/index.php/fnr/article/view/498). Some versions print "mg" for "µg", which looks like a typo.
- Vitamin D in salmon: farmed Atlantic salmon is quoted at 441 IU (about 11 µg) per 100 g, and wild salmon is said to be higher on average and to vary by catch location. — [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/nutrition/9-foods-high-in-vitamin-d). This is a consumer site and a weak source. No quantitative wild-versus-farmed range was found.

### Inferences
- Soil-driven trace elements (Se, and by extension I) show the widest ranges, from tens- to hundreds-fold. Intervention-driven vitamins (D2 with UV) are also very wide. Farm-system and season effects on a single product such as milk are typically 1.5-2.5-fold. These are inferences from a few foods.
- Within-batch SD for milk iodine is around 25 µg/L on a 70-110 µg/L mean (CV roughly 25-35%). Between-season and between-system differences are comparable in size to that scatter.

### Gaps
- USDA FoodData Central/SR Legacy/NFNAP n, SE, min and max figures, and CV % for vitamin C, carotenoids, folate, Fe, Zn, Ca, Mg and K, were not retrieved. No search targeted them.
- No numbers on folate in leafy greens, carotenoids by cultivar, or vitamin E.

## 3. Causes of variability and which dominate

### Takeaway
Dominant causes depend on the nutrient. Soil chemistry and pH drive Se. Feed, supplementation, season and iodine-based hygiene products drive milk I. UV dose drives mushroom vitamin D. Genotype, light, nitrogen, maturity and post-harvest temperature drive vitamin C. Breeding for yield may dilute mineral and vitamin concentrations.

### Cited Findings
- Vitamin C in horticultural crops: genotype, pre-harvest climate, cultural practices, maturity, harvest method and post-harvest handling all affect it. Higher light intensity is linked to higher vitamin C, and high nitrogen tends to lower it. After harvest, losses rise with temperature and storage time, though some chilling-sensitive crops lose more at low temperature. Mechanical damage lowers retention. Dehydroascorbic acid is usually under 10% of total vitamin C but rises in storage. — [Lee & Kader 2000 (UCANR PDF)](https://ucanr.edu/datastoreFiles/608-371.pdf); [Aprifel summary](https://www.aprifel.com/fr/resume-scientifique/preharvest-and-postharvest-factors-influencing-vitamin-c/). I read only abstract-level excerpts.
- Dilution effect: Davis, Epp & Riordan (2004, J Am Coll Nutr) compared USDA tables for 43 garden crops, 1950 versus 1999. Median declines of 6% to 38% were reported for protein, Ca, P, Fe, riboflavin and vitamin C. The authors attribute much of this to breeding for yield and growth rate. Davis calls the evidence preliminary, and it rests on historical table comparisons, not controlled experiments. The analytical methods and the sampled cultivars may also have changed between the two dates. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/soil-depletion-and-nutrition-loss/); [Pulse Brain summary](https://vitagri.org/evidence/XL0917/). Both are secondary sources.
- Broccoli calcium: 130 mg (1950) to 48 mg (later USDA data), per press coverage of Davis. — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-news/nutritional-value-fruits-veggies-dwindling-flna1c9457205). Secondary source.
- Mushroom vitamin D2: roughly linear with UV dose up to 1000 mJ/cm². Fast growth diluted D2 early, and storage and cooking reduce it. — [Food & Nutrition Research 2012](https://foodandnutritionresearch.net/index.php/fnr/article/view/498)
- Milk iodine: season (lower in summer and autumn), farm type, mineral supplements and teat disinfection with iodine products all contribute, and the organic-versus-conventional effect is inconsistent across studies. — [ETH Zurich](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/247182); [SARE tables](https://projects.sare.org/media/pdf/T/a/b/Tables-11-13.pdf)
- Brazil nut Se: soil total Se and soil acidity (acidity reduces uptake). — [Chemosphere 2017 record](https://repositorio.unesp.br/handle/11449/163410?show=full)

### Inferences
- Because the Davis comparison confounds cultivar, method and sampling changes, it is better treated as suggestive than as proof of a declining trend.

### Gaps
- No quantified vitamin C loss during storage (for example oranges, tomatoes or potatoes).
- No quantified carotenoid variation by cultivar or colour.
- No quantified folate variation in leafy greens.
- No quantified effects of fortification, cooking or analytical method.
- No primary source for the Davis et al. 2004 paper itself, and none for the effect of fertilisation on Fe or Zn.

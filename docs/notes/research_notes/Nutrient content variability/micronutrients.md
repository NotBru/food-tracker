# Variability of vitamins and minerals per unit weight within the same food, and its causes

Method note: every figure below was read from the paper's full text (Lee & Kader 2000, Lu et al. 2007, Mayer et al. 2021) or from its abstract in Europe PMC or the publisher/repository record. I could not read the full text of Davis et al. 2004, which is paywalled and has no open-access copy (Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex both list it as "closed"), so its findings come from the abstract only. "My calculation" marks numbers I derived; the papers do not state them.

## 1. Vitamin C: losses during storage and retail, and differences between cultivars

### Takeaway
Storage temperature is the main postharvest driver of vitamin C loss, and water loss (wilting) speeds it up. Leafy greens lose vitamin C fastest. Kale lost about 2–5% in 2 days at 0 °C but 61–89% in 2 days at 20 °C. Crucifers such as broccoli and Brussels sprouts keep more than 95%, while asparagus and green beans keep only 5–30%. Cultivar ranges at harvest are usually 2.5–4-fold (strawberry 19.3–71.5 mg/100 g; pepper 64–168 mg/100 g). Maturity, light and nitrogen each shift vitamin C by roughly 10–40%.

### Cited Findings
**Lee & Kader 2000 (read in full).** Lee SK, Kader AA. *Postharvest Biol Technol* 20:207–220. DOI 10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2. [PDF (UCANR, archived copy)](https://ucanr.edu/datastoreFiles/608-371.pdf)
- **Main factors.** Lee & Kader call temperature management after harvest "the most important factor" for keeping vitamin C. Losses speed up with higher temperature and longer storage. However, some chilling-sensitive crops lose more vitamin C at low temperatures. — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Kale, Table 5 (Ezell & Wilcox 1959).** Average vitamin C loss after 2 days:
  - Slow wilting: 2.4% at 0 °C, 15.3% at 10 °C, 60.9% at 20 °C.
  - Moderate wilting: 3.8%, 15.8% and 69.6%.
  - Rapid wilting: 5.3%, 33.1% and 88.8%.
  - The PDF text extraction scrambled the column order. I re-assigned the columns so each row rises with temperature. — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Leafy vegetables (Zepplin & Elvehjem 1944).** At 6 °C they lost 10% of ascorbic acid in 6 days. At room temperature they lost 20% in only 2 days. Spinach, cabbage and snap beans gave similar results (Ezell & Wilcox 1959). — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Tomatoes.** A 24 h delay between harvest and processing cost about 5% of ascorbic acid at 30 °C and 12% at 40 °C (Kader & Morris 1978). — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Broccoli cultivars.** Retention after 3 weeks at 2 °C ranged from 56% to 98% across six cultivars (Albrecht et al. 1990). Wu et al. (1992) found ascorbic acid stable in broccoli at 5 °C, but falling fast in green beans after 3 days. — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Retention classes by vegetable.** Lee & Kader classify vegetables by ascorbic acid retention during storage:
  - High (>95%): broccoli and Brussels sprouts.
  - Medium (65–70%): green pea, spinach and turnip.
  - Low (5–30%): asparagus and green beans.
  - Retention was linked to high sulphur and glutathione content. — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Strawberries.** Unwrapped berries lost 20–30% of total ascorbic acid over 8 days at 1 °C. Wrapped berries changed little over 8 days at 1 °C or 10 °C, because wrapping prevented water loss (Nunes et al. 1998). — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Fresh-cut kiwifruit.** Total vitamin C fell 8%, 13% and 21% after 6 days at 0, 5 and 10 °C (Agar et al. 1999). — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Long cold storage of apples.** After 6 months at 0 °C, Delicious fell from 10.2 to 4.2 mg/100 g. McIntosh fell from 5.0 mg/100 g to traces (Zubeckis 1962). — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Chilling injury.** In chilling-sensitive crops, vitamin C losses accelerate at low temperature:
  - Cucumber ascorbic acid fell continuously at 5 °C but not at 20 °C.
  - Sweet potato lost ascorbic acid only in chilling-browned tissue (Izumi et al. 1984). — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Cutting.** Shredding lettuce by machine left 25–63% less ascorbic acid than tearing it by hand. A blunt blade gave 10% less than a sharp one (Barry-Ryan & O'Beirne 1999). — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Cultivar ranges.** Six strawberry cultivars from four locations ranged from 19.3 to 71.5 mg/100 g (Nelson et al. 1972). Five fresh pepper cultivars ranged from 64 to 168 mg/100 g (Lee et al. 1995). In citrus, the flavedo (outer peel) holds four times as much ascorbic acid as the juice. — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Region (Table 1, Vanderslice et al. 1990).** Total vitamin C was 83.2 mg/100 g in California oranges and 63.0 mg/100 g in Florida oranges. Coastal California grapefruit had more than desert-grown grapefruit. 'Frost Satsuma' mandarins grown cool had more than those grown hot. — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Nitrogen.** Raising N from 80 to 120 kg/ha cut vitamin C in cauliflower by 7%. In potato cultivars, response to N varied (Augustin 1975, Table 3). Russet Burbank went from 130.7 to 91.8 to 111.7 mg/100 g dry weight at 45, 135 and 225 kg N/ha. Lee & Kader propose a growth "dilution effect" and shading by extra foliage as the mechanisms. — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Light and maturity.**
  - Fruit on the sunlit outside of the plant contains more vitamin C than shaded fruit.
  - Red pepper has about 30% more total vitamin C than green pepper.
  - Tomatoes picked at the 'breaker' stage reached only 69% of the ascorbic acid of vine-ripened fruit.
  - Tomatoes picked table-ripe had 19.2 mg/100 g; those picked mature-green and ripened at 20 °C had 12.3 mg/100 g.
  - Citrus is highest in concentration when immature. — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)
- **Dehydroascorbic acid (DHA).** In many crops DHA is less than 10% of total vitamin C, but it rises during storage. — [Lee & Kader 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-5214(00)00133-2)

**Other primary studies**
- **Packaged fresh spinach.** Only 53% of folate remained after 8 days at 4 °C, 6 days at 10 °C or 4 days at 20 °C, which were the points where the spinach became visually unacceptable. Carotenoid retention at those points was 54%, 61% and 44%. Packaging made no difference. — [Pandrangi & LaBorde 2004, J Food Sci](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=Pandrangi%20LaBorde%20folate&resultType=core&format=json)
- **Fresh-cut spinach.** Starting vitamin C was 750 mg/kg fresh weight. Modified-atmosphere packaging (MAP) kept more vitamin C than air, and ascorbic acid converted to DHA during storage. Boiling moved 60% of the vitamin C into the cooking water. — [Gil, Ferreres & Tomás-Barberán 1999, J Agric Food Chem, DOI 10.1021/jf981200l](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf981200l)
- **Broccoli and cauliflower, harvest to freezer.**
  - At harvest, broccoli had 116.3–116.4 mg/100 g and cauliflower 60.5–64.7 mg/100 g.
  - Blanching was the biggest processing loss: 41–42% for broccoli and 28–32% for cauliflower.
  - Frozen storage for 12 months cost a further 15–18% (broccoli) and 6–13% (cauliflower).
  - Raising N from 80 to 120 kg/ha lowered vitamin C only in cauliflower, by 7%. — [Lisiewska & Kmiecik 1996, Food Chem, DOI 10.1016/0308-8146(95)00218-9](https://doi.org/10.1016/0308-8146(95)00218-9)
- **Fresh versus frozen (UK).** Frozen peas and broccoli matched typical market-bought fresh produce and beat peas stored at home for several days. Fresh peas and broccoli kept their quality for up to 14 days chilled. Frozen spinach was "clearly superior to all market produce". The abstract gives no per-day percentages. — [Favell 1998, Food Chem, DOI 10.1016/S0308-8146(97)00165-9](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0308-8146(97)00165-9)
- **Potatoes in cold storage.** Twelve clones were held at 4 °C for 7 months. Vitamin C was highest at harvest and at 2 months, then "dropped rapidly" in every clone. Yukon Gold was consistently highest. The abstract gives no percentages. — [Külen, Stushnoff & Holm 2013, J Sci Food Agric, DOI 10.1002/jsfa.6053](https://doi.org/10.1002/jsfa.6053)
- **Baby spinach under shade nets.** Shading lowered ascorbic acid by 12–33% in April-sown spinach, with an inconsistent effect in August-sown spinach. — [Bergquist et al. 2007, J Agric Food Chem, DOI 10.1021/jf070396z](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf070396z)
- **Orange juice concentrate.** Vitamin C degradation rate constants ran from 4.79×10⁻⁴ to 3.13×10⁻² per week at 4–24 °C. — [Lee & Chen 1998, J Agric Food Chem, DOI 10.1021/jf980248w](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf980248w)

### Inferences
- Approximate per-day loss rates for leafy greens, from the figures above (my calculation):
  - About 1–2.5%/day at 0 °C (kale).
  - About 1.7%/day at 6 °C (leafy vegetables).
  - About 8–16%/day at 10 °C (kale).
  - About 10%/day at room temperature (leafy vegetables).
  - About 30–45%/day at 20 °C with wilting (kale).
- Temperature combined with humidity can therefore matter more than cultivar over a typical week of home or retail storage.
- Losses differ by crop more than by temperature in some cases. Broccoli held 56–98% after 3 weeks at 2 °C, while green beans lost ascorbic acid within 3 days at 5 °C. A single "% loss per day" therefore cannot be applied across vegetables.
- Folate in spinach falls at a rate similar to vitamin C. Losing about half by the visual end of shelf life means losses run at roughly 6%/day at 4 °C and 12%/day at 20 °C (my calculation from Pandrangi & LaBorde).

### Gaps
- I found no primary study giving a % loss per day for whole oranges stored as fruit, rather than as juice. Lee & Kader say only that citrus loses less than vegetables because ascorbic acid is more stable at acidic pH.
- I did not quantify potato vitamin C loss per month, because the Külen abstract has no numbers.
- Rickman et al. 2007 (J Sci Food Agric) is often cited for "spinach loses 100% at 20 °C in 7 days". I could not retrieve it from Europe PMC, so I have not verified that claim.
- Favell 1998 gives only qualitative results in its abstract.

## 2. Carotenoids, folate and vitamin E: cultivar ranges and effects of light and maturity

### Takeaway
Among standard orange cultivars, β-carotene varies about 2–2.5-fold (carrot 4.6–10.3 mg/100 g). Across flesh colours the range is far wider: sweet potato runs from trace to more than 9 mg/100 g. Folate in spinach varies about 3.2-fold across genotypes grown under identical conditions. Tomato lycopene is dominated by environment, with a broad-sense heritability of only 5.8%. Shade tends to raise carotenoids in leaves even as it lowers vitamin C. I found no cultivar-range data for vitamin E.

### Cited Findings
- **Carrot.** Across 19 orange cultivars, analysed by HPLC:
  - β-carotene: 4,600–10,300 µg/100 g fresh weight.
  - α-carotene: 2,200–4,900 µg/100 g.
  - γ-carotene: 630–2,700 µg/100 g.
  - Lutein: 110–560 µg/100 g.
  - Provitamin A: 1,200–2,300 µg retinol equivalents (RE)/100 g. — [Heinonen 1990, J Agric Food Chem, DOI 10.1021/jf00093a005](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf00093a005)
- **Dark orange carrot.** It had "twice as much carotene as any other variety" tested. — [Simon & Wolff 1987, J Agric Food Chem, DOI 10.1021/jf00078a038](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf00078a038)
- **Coloured carrots over two seasons.** White, yellow and purple cultivars had low carotenoids, and only the red cultivar had lycopene. Carotenoids "varied slightly" between 2003 and 2004. α-carotene differed noticeably between years in orange types, and lycopene in the red cultivar was higher in 2004. — [Grassmann, Schnitzler & Habegger 2007, Int J Food Sci Nutr, DOI 10.1080/09637480701359149](https://doi.org/10.1080/09637480701359149)
- **Sweet potato.**
  - Total carotenoids ranged from trace to more than 9 mg β-carotene equivalents/100 g fresh root, depending on flesh colour.
  - Drying at 65 °C for 12 h cut carotenoids by 30%.
  - Eleven months of storage as dried chips cost another 10%. — [Hagenimana et al. 1999, Ecol Food Nutr](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=Hagenimana%20carotenoid%20sweetpotato&resultType=core&format=json)
- **Orange-fleshed sweet potato germplasm.** β-carotene stayed stable during growth at about 4.56, 9.09 and 11.54 mg/100 g in three germplasms. A fourth rose from 4.99 to 7.25 mg/100 g as the roots grew. — [Ma et al. 2026, Front Plant Sci, DOI 10.3389/fpls.2026.1744016](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2026.1744016)
- **Sweet potato across sites.** In 22 genotypes grown at 4 locations over 2 seasons, the genotype × environment interaction was significant for β-carotene, Fe and Zn. — [Chizhande et al. 2026, Plant-Environ Interact, DOI 10.1002/pei3.70155](https://doi.org/10.1002/pei3.70155)
- **Tomato.** In 44 vintage varieties grown in 5 environments, genotype, environment and G×E were all significant for lycopene and vitamin C. Broad-sense heritability ranged from 5.8% for lycopene to 35.7% for titratable acidity. — [Panthee et al. 2013, Euphytica, DOI 10.1007/s10681-013-0895-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10681-013-0895-1)
- **Folate in spinach.** Across 67 accessions grown under controlled conditions, total folate ranged from 54.1 to 173.2 µg/100 g fresh weight, a 3.2-fold spread that depended on the accession. The main form was 5-CH₃-H₄-folate, averaging up to 52% of the total. — [Shohag et al. 2011, J Agric Food Chem, DOI 10.1021/jf203442h](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf203442h)
- **Folate and carotenoid losses in storage.** Retention fell to 53% for folate and 44–61% for carotenoids by the end of spinach shelf life (see section 1). — [Pandrangi & LaBorde 2004](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=Pandrangi%20LaBorde%20folate&resultType=core&format=json)
- **Light.** In baby spinach, shade netting significantly raised total carotenoids and chlorophylls in many cases, especially under low-transmittance and spectrum-altering nets. The same nets lowered ascorbic acid by 12–33% in April-sown spinach. — [Bergquist et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf070396z)
- **Vitamin E.** The US NFNAP study measured tocopherols in 32 fruits and 22 vegetables. α-tocopherol was usually the main form. γ-tocopherol exceeded α-tocopherol in cantaloupe, figs, raspberries, cauliflower, mushrooms, lettuce and green peas. Tocotrienols were usually below 0.1 mg/100 g. The abstract does not report within-food variation. — [Chun et al. 2006, J Food Compos Anal, DOI 10.1016/j.jfca.2005.08.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfca.2005.08.001)

### Inferences
- For provitamin A, the choice of cultivar or colour type is the dominant source of variation. White-to-orange differences are 100-fold or more; differences among orange types are 2–2.5-fold.
- For lycopene, growing conditions and ripeness matter more than cultivar among similar red types, given the 5.8% heritability.
- Light pushes leaf carotenoids and vitamin C in opposite directions, so the micronutrient profile of a leafy green depends on its growing conditions.

### Gaps
- I found no primary vitamin E (tocopherol) cultivar-range data for common foods.
- I found no tomato lycopene range in mg/100 g across cultivars from an abstract I could verify.
- Folate variation in other leafy greens (lettuce, kale) was not found.

## 3. Minerals: soil and fertiliser effects, the dilution effect, and the historical-decline studies

### Takeaway
Minerals in food that depend on the soil can vary by tens of times:
- Brazil-nut selenium medians differ about 33-fold between Amazon states.
- UK wheat selenium is very low, about 0.025–0.033 mg/kg.
- Finnish selenium fertilisation raised cereal Se about 15-fold.
- Retail milk iodine differs 30–45% between conventional and organic.

Zinc and iron in wheat vary among genotypes. Grain zinc has fallen with modern high-yield cultivars, a genetic "dilution effect" shown with archived Broadbalk samples while soil levels stayed flat or rose. The historical table comparisons (Mayer 1997; Davis et al. 2004; Mayer et al. 2021) show median declines of roughly 5–50%, depending on the nutrient. Their own authors and Marles (2017) warn that such comparisons are confounded by changes in method, cultivar and sampling.

### Cited Findings
**Davis, Epp & Riordan 2004 (abstract only; full text closed-access).** *J Am Coll Nutr* 23(6):669–682. DOI 10.1080/07315724.2004.10719409. PMID 15637215.
- **What it measured.** The authors compared USDA nutrient data published in 1950 and 1999 for 13 nutrients plus water in 43 garden crops, mostly vegetables. They adjusted for moisture differences, then calculated a ratio R (1999/1950) for each food and nutrient. They summarised the group with median and geometric-mean R values. Confidence intervals for individual R values were "hypothetical": they were built from USDA's 1999 standard errors plus two estimates of the 1950 standard errors. No new samples were analysed. — [Davis et al. 2004 (Europe PMC abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1080/07315724.2004.10719409)
- **Results.** As a group, the foods showed "statistically reliable declines" in 6 nutrients: protein, Ca, P, Fe, riboflavin and ascorbic acid. The other 7 nutrients showed no reliable change. Median declines ranged from 6% (protein) to 38% (riboflavin). — [Davis et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1080/07315724.2004.10719409)
- **Limitations stated in the abstract.**
  - For individual foods and nutrients, R values "are usually not distinguishable from 1 with current data".
  - Depending on whether low or high 1950 standard-error estimates are used, only 33% or 20% of R values differ reliably from 1.
  - About 28% of those that do differ are above 1, meaning increases.
  - The authors say only that "any real declines are generally most easily explained by changes in cultivated varieties", with possible trade-offs between yield and nutrient content. — [Davis et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1080/07315724.2004.10719409)
- **Davis's later review (Davis 2009, HortScience 44:15).** It groups three lines of evidence: early fertilisation studies, historical table comparisons with median declines of about 5–40% in some minerals, and side-by-side trials of old and new cultivars. Davis calls historical table comparisons "inherently limited". This comes from the abstract only. — [Davis 2009 abstract (remineralize.org mirror)](https://remineralize.org/?p=13151)

**Mayer 1997 (abstract).** *Br Food J* 99(6):207–211. DOI 10.1108/00070709710181540.
- **What it measured and found.** Mayer compared 20 fruits and 20 vegetables in UK *Composition of Foods* tables from the 1930s (3rd edition) and the 1980s (5th edition), on a fresh-weight basis.
  - Significant falls in vegetables: Ca, Mg, Cu and Na.
  - Significant falls in fruit: Mg, Fe, Cu and K.
  - P was the only mineral with no significant change.
  - Fruit water content rose and dry matter fell.
  - Mayer lists possible causes as "anomalies of measurement or sampling, changes in the food system, changes in the varieties grown or changes in agricultural practice". — [Mayer 1997](https://doi.org/10.1108/00070709710181540)

**Mayer, Trenchard & Rayns 2021 (read in full).** *Int J Food Sci Nutr*. DOI 10.1080/09637486.2021.1981831.
- **Data.** The authors used 29 fruits and vegetables that match across the 1940, 1991 and 2019 UK tables, adjusted to dry weight.
- **Changes 1940–2019:**
  - Na −51.7%, Fe −50.2%, Cu −49.1% and Mg −9.7% (all significant).
  - K −4.7% and Ca −2.5% (not significant).
  - P +0.5%.
  - Water +1.4% and dry matter −11.6%.
- **By period.** From 1940 to 1991, Cu fell 60.1%. From 1991 to 2019, Fe fell 35.5% (p = 0.001) while Mg rose 17.8%. — [Mayer et al. 2021 (Coventry repository PDF)](https://pure.coventry.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/45908836/Mayer_et_al_Historical_changes_mineral_content.pdf)

**Marles 2017 critique.** *J Food Compos Anal*. DOI 10.1016/j.jfca.2016.11.012.
- **Why table comparisons are unreliable.** Marles says comparisons of composition tables published decades apart "are not reliable". Data sources, varieties, geographic origin, ripeness, sample size, sampling methods, laboratory analysis and statistical treatment all change over time. — [Marles 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfca.2016.11.012)
- **Soil depletion.** Archived soils show no decline in soil minerals under intensive cultivation. — [Marles 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfca.2016.11.012)
- **What does hold up.** Old and new varieties grown side by side show lower mineral concentrations in high-yield varieties, a "dilution effect". — [Marles 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfca.2016.11.012)
- **Scale of the copper decline.** The extreme apparent copper declines of −34% to −81% are small in absolute terms against natural ranges. Copper per 100 g dry weight runs 0.11–1.71 mg in vegetables, 0.01–2.06 mg in fruit (the abstract prints "01") and 0.1–1.4 mg in grains. Marles describes these as natural ranges of variation of 1,555%, 20,600% and 1,400%. — [Marles 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfca.2016.11.012)

**Dilution effect in archived and contemporaneous samples**
- **Broadbalk wheat, 1845 onwards.** Archived samples show grain Zn, Fe, Cu and Mg stable from 1845 to the mid-1960s, then falling significantly once semi-dwarf high-yield cultivars came in. Soil concentrations rose or held steady over the same period. The same fall appeared in plots with no fertiliser, inorganic fertiliser or manure. Increasing yield and harvest index explained the decline. — [Fan et al. 2008, J Trace Elem Med Biol 22:315–324, DOI 10.1016/j.jtemb.2008.07.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtemb.2008.07.002)
- **HEALTHGRAIN wheat lines.** The study analysed 150 bread wheat lines and 25 durum, spelt, einkorn and emmer lines, plus 26 lines grown at 6 sites or seasons.
  - Grain Fe, Zn and Se varied substantially across lines.
  - Fe and Zn differed significantly between genotypes; Se did not, being "influenced more by the soil supply".
  - Grain Zn correlated negatively with yield and fell with variety release date.
  - Zn and Fe both correlated positively with grain protein and P.
  - The abstract gives no numeric ranges. — [Zhao et al. 2009, J Cereal Sci, DOI 10.1016/j.jcs.2008.11.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcs.2008.11.007)
- **Indian and Turkish bread wheats.** Across 150 genotypes, the authors report "several fold variations among genotypes" for almost all elements. — [Pandey et al. 2016, SpringerPlus, DOI 10.1186/s40064-016-3586-2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40064-016-3586-2)

**Selenium: soil and country differences**
- **Brazil nuts.** Median Se ranged from 2.07 mg/kg (Mato Grosso) to 68.15 mg/kg (Amazonas), so one nut supplies 11% to 288% of a 70 µg/day requirement. Soil total Se ranged from below 65.76 to 625.91 µg/kg. Nut Se rose with soil Se and fell in acidic soils. — [Silva Junior et al. 2017, Chemosphere 188:650–658, DOI 10.1016/j.chemosphere.2017.08.158](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemosphere.2017.08.158)
- **UK wheat.** National surveys found mean grain Se of 0.025 mg/kg (1982, n=180), 0.033 mg/kg (1992, n=187) and 0.025 mg/kg (1998, n=85) dry weight, with no long-term trend. Eastern England was higher than other regions. Adding more sulphur fertiliser lowered grain Se. — [Adams et al. 2002, J Sci Food Agric, DOI 10.1002/jsfa.1167](https://doi.org/10.1002/jsfa.1167)
- **Finland.** Fertilisers have contained Se since 1985 (now 15 mg Se/kg).
  - Se rose on average 15-fold in spring cereals, 6-fold in beef, 2-fold in pork and 3-fold in milk.
  - Organically grown plant foods match pre-1985 levels.
  - Milk from organically fed cows has 50% less Se than ordinary milk. — [Alfthan et al. 2015, J Trace Elem Med Biol, DOI 10.1016/j.jtemb.2014.04.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtemb.2014.04.009)
- **Why Finnish soil Se is poorly available.** Low soil Se, low pH and high iron limit uptake. Mean intake tripled within 2 years of fertilisation starting. When Se dosing was cut in 1990, intake fell 30%. — [Aro, Alfthan & Varo 1995, Analyst, DOI 10.1039/an9952000841](https://doi.org/10.1039/an9952000841)

**Iodine in milk, eggs and fish (feed-driven)**
- **UK retail milk, winter.**
  - Study 1: organic milk had 32.2% less iodine than conventional (404 vs 595 µg/L).
  - Study 2: 35.5% less (306 vs 474 µg/L).
  - UHT milk had 331 µg/L, and branded organic milk 268 µg/L. — [Payling et al. 2015, Food Chem, DOI 10.1016/j.foodchem.2015.01.091](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2015.01.091)
- **UK retail milk, follow-up.** Organic milk had 44% less iodine than conventional (241 vs 427 µg/L), and UHT 27% less (314 µg/L). The gaps were smaller in summer. — [Stevenson, Drake & Givens 2018, Food Chem, DOI 10.1016/j.foodchem.2017.06.135](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2017.06.135)
- **Norway.** Cow milk had 12–19 µg/100 g iodine and hen's eggs 23–43 µg/100 g. Wild fish ranged from 18 µg/100 g (Atlantic halibut) to 1,210 µg/100 g (pollack). The values differ from the Norwegian food composition table. — [Nerhus et al. 2018, Food Nutr Res, DOI 10.29219/fnr.v62.1291](https://doi.org/10.29219/fnr.v62.1291)
- **Switzerland, US Northeast and a global review.** The earlier lite notes (since removed; in git history at commit 6dfdbd4) cited Swiss (Agroscope, ETH), US Northeast and systematic-review figures for milk iodine. Their main points: winter milk is higher than summer, conventional higher than organic, and iodine teat dips add iodine. I did not re-verify these in this run.

### Inferences
- Selenium is the clearest case where soil, not genotype, sets the content. Zhao et al. found no genotype effect on wheat Se, and the soil-dependent ranges run from 10-fold to more than 100-fold.
- Zinc and iron vary several-fold by genotype, and Zn is subject to yield dilution.
- Iodine in animal foods is set mainly by feed and mineral supplementation, and in milk also by iodine teat dips. Farm system (organic vs conventional) and season produce 30–45% differences in retail milk.
- The historical-decline literature is consistent in direction for Cu, Fe, Mg and Na. However, the size of the change is confounded with changes in method and cultivar. Archived-sample work (Fan 2008) and side-by-side cultivar trials (Zhao 2009, cited by Marles 2017) are stronger evidence than table comparisons, and they point to dilution by yield rather than soil depletion.

### Gaps
- I could not read the full text of Davis et al. 2004. Its detailed limitations section is therefore not recorded; only the limitations stated in the abstract are. Examples of what is missing are the 1950 analytical methods, the treatment of outliers, and the cultivar-change argument.
- No numeric Zn and Fe ranges for wheat or rice were obtained, since the Zhao 2009 and Pandey 2016 abstracts lack numbers.
- No primary source was found for North American wheat Se to set beside the UK figures. Combs 2001 (Br J Nutr) was located, but its abstract has no numbers.
- No primary data on iodine in vegetables was found, only old (1949) or regional (Romania) records without usable numbers.

## 4. Vitamin D in fish, eggs and UV-treated mushrooms; vitamin B12 and iodine in animal foods

### Takeaway
Vitamin D in foods is set by exposure, through the diet of fish, sunlight or feed for hens, and UV dose for mushrooms. Farmed salmon has about a quarter of the vitamin D of wild salmon, and wild fish vary enormously even within a species. Eggs from hens with outdoor access have 3–4 times more vitamin D3 in the yolk. Vitamin D2 in mushrooms goes from near zero to tens of µg/g dry solids with seconds-to-minutes of UV. Milk B12 varies about 13-fold between cows, driven by diet fibre, rumen conditions and genetics (heritability 0.37).

### Cited Findings
- **Salmon and other fish (Lu et al. 2007, read in full).** Vitamin D3, in IU per 3.5 oz (about 100 g), reported as mean ± SEM:
  - Wild salmon: 988 ± 524 (n=9).
  - Farmed salmon: 240 ± 108 (n=9).
  - Farmed trout: 388 ± 212 (n=4).
  - Tuna: 404 ± 440 (n=4).
  - Cod: 104 ± 24.
  - Grey sole: 56 ± 36.
  - Mackerel: 24 (n=1).
  - The authors state that farmed salmon had "approximately 25%" of wild salmon's vitamin D, and that "the vitamin D content in fish varied widely even within species".
  - Cooking: baking retained almost all (245 → 240 IU), while frying in vegetable oil kept about 50% (123 IU). — [Lu et al. 2007, J Steroid Biochem Mol Biol 103:642–644, DOI 10.1016/j.jsbmb.2006.12.010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2698592/)
- **Eggs: sunlight.** Hens were kept outdoors, indoors, or with a choice of both, for 4 weeks.
  - Yolk vitamin D3 averaged 14.3 µg/100 g dry matter (outdoor), 11.3 (indoor/outdoor) and 3.8 (indoor).
  - Free-range eggs bought from supermarkets had "relatively low" vitamin D. — [Kühn et al. 2014, Nutrition, DOI 10.1016/j.nut.2013.10.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nut.2013.10.002)
- **Eggs and chicken: feed.** Two Finnish commercial egg-yolk pools contained 4.9 ± 0.14 and 4.0 ± 0.10 µg vitamin D3/100 g, plus 1.0–1.3 µg/100 g of 25-OH-D3. Chicken meat had 0.2–0.3 µg D3/100 g. 25-OH-D3 in the hens' feed transfers efficiently to the yolk. — [Mattila, Valkonen & Valaja 2011, J Agric Food Chem, DOI 10.1021/jf2012634](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf2012634)
- **Mushrooms: UV-C.** Fresh button mushrooms exposed for 2.5, 5 and 10 min reached 6.6, 15.6 and 23.1 µg vitamin D2/g solids, equal to 40.6–141 µg per serving. D2 was stable over 8 days at 4 °C and at room temperature (P = 0.36). — [Koyyalamudi et al. 2009, J Agric Food Chem, DOI 10.1021/jf803908q](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf803908q)
- **Mushrooms: UV-B.** UV dose had the largest effect: 0.5 J/cm² gave about 3.8 µg D2/g dry solids. In treated mushrooms, D2 then degraded by first-order kinetics with k = 0.025 h⁻¹, a half-life of about 28 h (my calculation). — [Roberts, Teichert & McHugh 2008, J Agric Food Chem, DOI 10.1021/jf0732511](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf0732511)
  - This conflicts with Koyyalamudi 2009, which found no D2 loss over 8 days.
- **Mushrooms, earlier figure (not re-verified).** The earlier lite notes cite a range of 0.2 to 164 µg/100 g fresh weight for UV-treated *Agaricus*. — [Food & Nutrition Research 2012](https://foodandnutritionresearch.net/index.php/fnr/article/view/498)
- **Milk B12 across cows.** In 3,533 Holstein cows from 99 herds:
  - Milk B12 averaged 4.2 ng/mL, with a range of 0.7–9.0 ng/mL.
  - Dietary fibre raised it; energy-dense diet components (starch, concentrate) lowered it.
  - Heritability was 0.37.
  - The full model explained 79% of the variation. — [Duplessis et al. 2024, JDS Commun, DOI 10.3168/jdsc.2023-0535](https://doi.org/10.3168/jdsc.2023-0535)
- **Milk B12 and rumen conditions.** Ruminal pH and the acetate:propionate ratio correlated positively with milk B12. Bedding had no effect, and bulk-tank milk averaged 4,276 pg/mL. — [Duplessis et al. 2021, Animals, DOI 10.3390/ani11020532](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11020532)
- **Iodine in milk, eggs and fish.** See section 3: Payling 2015, Stevenson 2018 and Nerhus 2018. Milk and eggs vary with feed iodine and farm system.

### Inferences
- Coefficients of variation implied by Lu et al. 2007, if the "± SEM" values are SEMs (SD = SEM × √n), are huge (my calculation):
  - Wild salmon: SD about 1,570 IU, a CV of about 160%.
  - Farmed salmon: SD about 320 IU, a CV of about 135%.
  - If the values are actually SDs, the CVs are about 53% and 45%.
  - Either way, vitamin D in a single fish species varies more than almost any macronutrient does.
- For eggs and mushrooms, the production system (sun or UV exposure, feed) matters more than biological variation. For milk B12, the cow's diet and genetics drive more than 10-fold differences between individual cows. Bulk retail milk would average much of this out.

### Gaps
- I found no primary study of B12 in eggs as driven by feed. The search returned only performance studies and analytical-method papers.
- I could not find a primary paper with between-sample ranges for wild salmon vitamin D larger than the n=9 in Lu et al. 2007. The Norwegian and Danish fish vitamin D surveys were not retrieved.

## 5. Is there a study directly comparing within-food CV for micronutrients versus macronutrients?

### Takeaway
I found no study that directly compares the within-food CV of micronutrients with that of macronutrients for the same samples. The indirect evidence points the same way. In historical comparisons, macronutrient and proximate values (protein, water, dry matter) changed by single digits to about 12%, while several minerals and vitamins changed by 30–60%. Exposure-driven micronutrients (vitamin D, Se, iodine, B12) show 10-fold to more than 100-fold ranges. Even macronutrient measurement has between-laboratory CVs of 3–84%, so analytical noise is itself a large part of apparent variability.

### Cited Findings
- **Within one dataset (Davis 2004).** Median declines were 6% for protein against 38% for riboflavin, with Fe, Ca, P and ascorbic acid in between. — [Davis et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1080/07315724.2004.10719409)
- **Within one dataset (Mayer 2021).** From 1940 to 2019, water changed +1.4% and dry matter −11.6%, while Fe changed −50.2%, Cu −49.1% and Na −51.7%. — [Mayer et al. 2021](https://pure.coventry.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/45908836/Mayer_et_al_Historical_changes_mineral_content.pdf)
- **Analytical noise for macronutrients.** Nineteen leading food laboratories analysed the same homogenised foods: egg powder, milk powder, rye and wheat flours, biscuits and French beans. Between-lab CVs were:
  - Protein: 2.8–6.4%.
  - Total fat: 5.4–54%.
  - Available carbohydrate: 9–27%.
  - Total dietary fibre: 23–84%. — [Hollman & Katan 1988, J Am Diet Assoc, DOI 10.1016/S0002-8223(21)02007-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0002-8223(21)02007-1)
- **Wheat across national databases (non-peer-reviewed).** Comparing 19 national food composition databases with USDA for raw wheat, energy deviated at most 10%, protein 16% and carbohydrate 22%. Fe and Ca showed "greater variability between countries", with outliers at about 3 times the mean (NZ iron). This is an industry-initiative web page with no peer-reviewed paper identified, so it is weak evidence. — [Sustainable Nutrition Initiative](https://sustainablenutritioninitiative.com/variation-in-global-food-composition-data/)
- **Natural copper ranges.** Marles gives natural copper ranges of 1,400% to 20,600% within food groups. — [Marles 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfca.2016.11.012)
- **Bioactives.** For flavan-3-ols, (−)-epicatechin and nitrate, compositional variability "of even the same foods" is so high that intake estimates from food composition data are unreliable. This is not about vitamins or minerals, but it is the closest systematic treatment of within-food variability I found. — [Ottaviani et al. 2024, eLife, DOI 10.7554/eLife.92941](https://doi.org/10.7554/elife.92941)

### Inferences
- Combining the sources above, a reasonable working picture is as follows:
  - Macronutrients in a given raw food typically vary within tens of percent.
  - Genotype-driven micronutrients (vitamin C, carotenoids, folate, Zn, Fe) vary about 2–4-fold across cultivars and lose 10–90% in storage or processing.
  - Exposure- or soil-driven micronutrients (Se, I, vitamin D, B12) vary 10–100-fold or more.
- This is a synthesis, not a measured head-to-head CV comparison.
- Because laboratory CVs for some macronutrients (fat, fibre) can exceed 50%, any comparison must separate analytical variance from true biological variance.

### Gaps
- No paper reporting matched CVs for macronutrients and micronutrients in the same food samples was found, for example in USDA NFNAP sampling reports, EuroFIR, or the INFOODS variability guidance. Greenfield & Southgate's FAO *Food Composition Data* handbook may discuss typical CVs, but I did not retrieve it.
